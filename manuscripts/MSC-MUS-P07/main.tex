\begin{abstract}
AI-assisted music creation may coincide with changes in everyday affect, but event-triggered assessments of creation alone provide no scheduled noncreation comparison. We propose a 21-day observational ecological momentary assessment (EMA) study with independently scheduled observation windows. Current valence and arousal are assessed at baseline and approximately one and three hours later. Natural creation during the first hour is contrasted with windows without creation within the same person, adjusting for baseline affect, intention, study day and local time. The primary endpoint is valence at the first follow-up. Secondary outcomes concern later valence and proximity to the participant's initial arousal goal. Exposure is self-selected; the contrast is associative rather than causal. Separate synthetic calibrations of the primary (32,800 datasets) and secondary (144,000 datasets) endpoints illustrate dependence on creation frequency and usable within-person contrasts. In the secondary calibration, maximum false-family rejection was 0.0445 and 0.0425 under missing completely at random (MCAR) and missing at random (MAR) response-stage scenarios, respectively, and 0.2905 under missing not at random (MNAR). A 14,400-dataset exploratory extension at larger sample sizes illustrates persistent selection bias and coefficient bounds that include zero. No recruitment target is approved and no participant data or empirical results are available.
\end{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} affect dynamics; valence; arousal; ecological momentary assessment; generative AI; music creation; within-person association.
\section{Introduction}
The circumplex account distinguishes affective pleasantness from activation \citep{russell1980}. A rise in activation is not intrinsically beneficial or harmful; its meaning depends on context and desired affect. Emotion regulation includes attempts to influence emotional experience, rather than uniformly maximising positive affect \citep{gross2015}. Everyday music research has used experience sampling to characterising situational variation in music-evoked emotion and listening motives \citep{juslin2008,randall2017}. These studies address listening rather than generative creation. AI songwriting adds iterative generation, selection and editing; observations of competition teams establish the relevance of creative iteration, not its effect on mood \citep{huang2020}.
\par Affective persistence and variability are distinguishable constructs \citep{kuppens2010,houben2015}. Sparse, irregularly spaced reports do not identify continuous-time emotional inertia without stronger assumptions. We therefore focus on fixed observation horizons rather than interpreting a raw autocorrelation as regulatory capacity. EMA reduces dependence on distant recall \citep{shiffman2008}; separation of within-person and between-person variation is essential \citep{curran2011}.
\subsection{From music engagement to everyday creative decisions}
AI-assisted creation may involve anticipation, effort, uncertainty, repeated revision and evaluation of the resulting music. These operations are distinct from listening to an existing piece. A person may initiate creation when already distressed, when expecting enjoyment or when an opportunity becomes available. The same apparent improvement in valence may therefore reflect selection into the activity, recovery from an earlier state, concurrent activities or consequences of creation. The design is intended to characterise temporally ordered associations, not to decide among these explanations on its own.

Longitudinal evidence also cautions against treating music engagement as an exogenous activity. In an eight-week observational study of 428 regular music users, \citet{koehler2024} found that weeks with greater negative affect predicted greater subsequent use of both listening and music making for mood regulation. The reverse association with later negative affect was observed for time spent listening, but not for time spent making music. Their weekly assessment interval, conventional music activities and positive and negative affect measures differ from the hourly valence contrast in P07. The findings therefore support investigating temporal selection and competing directions of association, not predicting the magnitude or direction of the AI creation coefficient.

In generative music creation, the opportunity to externalise an experience, revise a representation or explore alternative musical expressions may be rewarding. Conversely, unwanted outputs, repeated unsuccessful generation or a mismatch between intention and result may be frustrating. These are candidate pathways rather than established mediators. The binary exposure does not separate them, and the proposed models do not test whether any pathway explains a change in affect.

Valence and arousal are complementary dimensions of subjective affect. A creator may value an activating experience or seek calm; neither low nor high activation is universally preferable. H3 concerns correspondence with a previously stated activation goal, which is not identical to emotional clarity, psychological ownership or goal attainment in a broader sense. This distinction prevents interpreting all movement towards a pleasant state as successful regulation. The goal can change within a session; the endpoint specifically uses the initial goal and cannot distinguish goal revision from failure to reach it.

The motivational account of emotion regulation distinguishes immediate experiential preferences from goals for which an emotional state has instrumental value \citep{tamir2016}. Applied to P07, this distinction permits an initial preference for elevated activation during creative work even if subsequent pleasantness is unchanged. The absolute distance to a stated activation goal is a narrow measure of goal correspondence; it does not demonstrate that a motive was fulfilled or that an intentional regulation strategy was effective. Creation may also occur for nonregulatory reasons. H1 and H3 therefore represent distinct associational questions rather than interchangeable indicators of psychological benefit.

\subsection{Why scheduled observation windows?}
A meta-analysis of 496 EMA samples from 477 articles reported mean compliance of approximately 79\% across heterogeneous designs \citep{wrzus2023}. This is a contextual benchmark, not an expected rate for P07 or evidence that three daily assessments will be feasible. Event-contingent reports of successful creation alone omit ordinary noncreation moments, abandoned attempts and missed opportunities. Scheduling windows independently of creation provides a defined denominator but does not randomise exposure or ensure that actual responses represent all scheduled moments.

The review by \citet{stone2023} highlights unresolved issues in momentary self-report, including interpretation, comparison standards, participant training, selection, missingness and reliability. For P07, this motivates separate recording of prompt, submission and exposure times and explicit response-process checks. Ratings refer to the current experience at actual submission, not the remembered state at the scheduled prompt. Consequently, delaying submission can change the moment sampled even when the answer falls within the accepted interval. Narrow timing analyses assess sensitivity to this choice but may introduce a different selection process.

Emotion dynamics concerns the temporal organisation of affective experiences, including their duration and patterns of change \citep{kuppens2017}. The sampling interval is consequently part of the scientific question. A first-hour contrast and a later contrast may reflect different mixtures of ongoing creation, subsequent activities and endogenous affective fluctuation. Neither contrast estimates recovery speed, emotional inertia or a continuous trajectory. The selected horizons are pragmatic design choices requiring feasibility assessment, not empirically established timescales for AI music creation.

\section{Research questions and hypotheses}
The primary question is whether naturally occurring AI-assisted creation during the first hour of a prescheduled window is associated with first-follow-up valence among participants who contribute valid complete windows of both exposure types. Two secondary questions concern the later valence association and correspondence with the activation goal declared before the window. All three questions concern adjusted comparisons within a person, not comparisons between frequent and infrequent creators.

\begin{description}
\item[P07-H1 (primary)] The adjusted within-person coefficient for first-follow-up valence is positive ($\beta_1>0$). The null is $\beta_1=0$; a two-sided test permits detection of an association in the opposite direction.
\item[P07-H2 (secondary)] Is first-hour creation associated with valence at the three-hour follow-up? No directional prediction is imposed ($\beta_2\ne 0$ against $\beta_2=0$). Subsequent activity may differ; the coefficient is not a test of persistence of an isolated intervention.
\item[P07-H3 (secondary)] The adjusted within-person coefficient for first-follow-up absolute deviation from baseline desired arousal is negative ($\beta_3<0$), with additional adjustment for initial deviation. The null is $\beta_3=0$. No direction is assumed for arousal itself.
\end{description}
The coefficients refer to the outcome-specific samples of observed switchers defined in the statistical analysis. All tests are two-sided; H2 and H3 constitute the fixed Holm family. A significant association opposite to the predicted sign does not support H1 or H3. A nonsignificant coefficient is not evidence of equivalence or absence of association. Exploratory analyses describe valence and arousal profiles at the sampled occasions, listening-only windows, duration, later creation and heterogeneity; they do not establish continuous trajectories.

\subsection{Conceptual model and competing explanations}
The conceptual model separates four components: the pre-window state (valence, arousal, desired arousal and intention), the naturally selected first-hour activity, the state at each follow-up and the observation process. Stable person characteristics and time-varying context may relate to both activity and affect. Baseline affect and intention can precede the decision to create, while unobserved affective changes within the first hour may also influence that decision. Creation and subsequent activities may be associated with follow-up states. The observation process may depend on the activity or affect itself; conditioning on observed complete windows can therefore change the target association. This is a conceptual account of competing pathways, not an identified causal model or a mediation analysis.

The planned adjustment set consists of baseline valence, baseline arousal, intention, study day, local baseline hour and participant fixed effects, with initial goal deviation additionally included for H3. Neither post-baseline activities nor candidate emotional mechanisms are confirmatory adjustment variables. Desired arousal is part of the H3 outcome definition, not an additional H1 covariate. The distinction between self-selected creation and its observation prevents interpreting missing exposure as noncreation. The study is distinct from P01's assigned-task contrasts, P03's recalled motives, P06's functional-interference endpoint and P08's manipulation of creative agency. If participants are shared, disclose overlap and assess publication redundancy before submission.

\subsection{Smallest effect size of interest (SESOI)}
Before confirmatory recruitment, justify a positive threshold in raw valence points for H1 using the response-process evidence from cognitive interviews, the intended scientific interpretation of the rating anchors and the precision feasible under the pilot-informed exposure and missingness scenarios. Use the pilot to assess rating comprehension, usable variation and exposure support, not to choose the threshold from the observed association or its significance. Assess a range of candidate thresholds in simulation and document why the selected value is scientifically meaningful rather than merely detectable. The existing synthetic values are calibration inputs, not validated minimal important differences. The threshold is not a clinical benefit cutoff, and no numerical value is approved. Use separate justification if a meaningful threshold is later specified for H2 or H3. Freeze the threshold and recruitment target before confirmation; do not infer equivalence from a nonsignificant test. Any formal equivalence analysis would require a separately prespecified estimand and decision rule.

\section{Method}
\subsection{Participants and observation design}
Recruit consenting Polish-speaking adult AI music creators who report recent routine use. Do not prescribe creation, paid platform use or emotional material. During 21 days, issue one prescheduled prompt per day at a randomly selected minute within a participant-defined available time band, leaving at least 210 minutes (15 minutes for baseline submission and 195 minutes for follow-up). Freeze the schedule independently of affect and actual creation; record every scheduled window, including missed prompts. A frozen enrolment-derived schedule defines all 21 planned local dates and immutable window identifiers, using a fixed participant-defined availability band and IANA time zone. The analysis reconciles responses against this schedule and preserves windows without response records. An offset-aware cessation log separates cancelled prompts at or after withdrawal from missed active prompts. Partial pre-withdrawal responses in an initiated window remain subject to approved retention rules; responses at or after cessation are invalid. Baseline is valid within 15 minutes of the prompt. Follow-up prompts are scheduled at 60 and 180 minutes from actual baseline submission; accept 60--75 and 180--195 minutes. Do not open follow-ups early. Participants may skip any response. The maximum burden is 21 daily windows and 63 scheduled assessment opportunities conditional on timely baseline response and continued participation. Follow-up prompts depend on baseline submission; distinguish planned opportunities from actually issued prompts. Figure~\ref{fig:ema-window} summarises the timing contract.

\subsection{Eligibility and response procedures}
Eligibility is established before scheduling: adults able to understand the Polish study materials, reporting recent routine AI-assisted music creation, and able to use the approved response system within their chosen availability band. The exact recent-use screening interval and recruitment channels must be fixed with the team before the pilot; no minimum observed creation frequency is imposed retrospectively. Participants receive neutral instructions and practice with fictional examples before the first scheduled day. A missed window is not rescheduled in response to mood or creation. No activity is requested by the prompt. Any reminder policy and actual notification delivery logging must be specified and tested before field deployment.

\subsection{Exposure and outcome measurement}
\par The exposure is any active AI-assisted music creation (prompting, generating, choosing or editing outputs as part of a creative task) during the first 60 minutes after baseline, regardless of completion. Listening alone is not creation. Collect actual first-hour exposure at the first follow-up or a separate exposure check, with its own timestamp; a later recollection is exploratory only. Record whether creation was already ongoing at baseline, and exclude ongoing windows from the primary comparison. Record pre-window intention, baseline context, other music activity, and later creation separately. Uncertain exposure is missing, never non-creation. Do not use post-baseline activities as primary adjustment covariates.
\par Valence (0 very unpleasant, 10 very pleasant), arousal (0 very low activation, 10 very high activation) and desired arousal (same activation anchors) are proposed original single-item ratings, not validated clinical scales. The current state, not the emotional content of the song, is rated. Desired arousal is elicited before exposure. Assess interpretation through cognitive interviews and feasibility pilot. No proprietary questionnaire items are included. Valence and arousal are bounded ordinal responses treated as numerical scores for the prespecified linear projection. This is a modelling convention, not a demonstration of equal perceptual intervals. Assess endpoint use and within-person variation in the pilot; no post hoc exclusion based on restricted affect variation.

A known noncreation window requires an explicit negative first-hour report. An abandoned active attempt is creation, whereas passive playback or unattended generation without active engagement during the window is not sufficient. If the respondent cannot distinguish whether active engagement occurred within the specified interval, retain unknown exposure. A skipped ongoing-creation item does not establish eligibility. For the adjusted models, a valid baseline requires an observed negative ongoing-creation response and all three baseline predictors (valence, arousal and intention), in addition to timely submission. Missing desired arousal does not invalidate H1 or H2, but precludes the H3 endpoint. Missing first-follow-up valence does not preclude H2 or H3 when their own measurement conditions are satisfied. These rules correspond to the existing analysis contract; they do not require new exclusion rules.

\subsection{Measurement development and feasibility}
The Polish candidate items are administered individually, with no composite score or diagnostic interpretation. The pilot assesses comprehension of the constructs and time reference, consistency of the response process, response burden and observed score support. The distinction between current affect and the emotional content of a musical output is tested using neutral fictional examples. For these single-item measures, internal-consistency coefficients are not an appropriate reliability criterion. Repeated ratings during ordinary activity also cannot establish test-retest reliability independently of genuine state changes. Report the observed distribution, within-person variation and completion separately for each item; document any changes to wording and timing before the confirmatory version is frozen.

\section{Statistical analysis}
\subsection{Target association and interpretation}
Let $C_{it}$ indicate creation in the first hour, $V_{it,0}$ and $A_{it,0}$ denote baseline affect and $I_{it,0}$ pre-window intention (0--10). For the primary outcome $V_{it,60}$ use
\[
V_{it,60}=\alpha_i+\beta C_{it}+\gamma V_{it,0}+
\delta A_{it,0}+\theta I_{it,0}+\eta d_{it}+\lambda h_{it}+\epsilon_{it},
\]
where $d$ is study day and $h$ is local baseline hour in decimal hours. Restrict the initial analysis to valid complete windows of participants with both exposure conditions represented, so $\beta$ describes the adjusted within-person linear projection in observed switchers. The target is not an average treatment effect or all creators. Fixed effects remove stable additive person-level differences, not unmeasured time-varying influences. The pooled coefficient weights participants according to their residualised exposure information, rather than assigning equal weight to each participant-specific association. With heterogeneous slopes, interpret it as a within-person projection in the retained sample. Do not equate it with the average individual slope. A coefficient of 0.4 denotes an adjusted .4-point difference on the rating scale, not a standardised effect, a clinical threshold or a change score. The pilot will assess response interpretation and support; the smallest meaningful association must be justified independently of significance. Use participant fixed effects, CR1 cluster standard errors with participant intercepts counted in degrees of freedom, and $t$ inference with $G-1$ degrees of freedom. Report coefficient and 95\% interval; require at least ten switcher clusters and full design rank as computational minima, not adequate sample size. H2 substitutes three-hour valence; H3 substitutes absolute arousal-goal deviation and includes baseline deviation. Holm adjustment covers H2 and H3; each uses its outcome-specific complete switcher subset. Report counts of complete windows and switcher participants, and compare the common complete subset descriptively. For each endpoint report all enrolled participants as no complete known window, noncreation only, creation only, or both, and compare this with support among valid baselines with known exposure. Observed switchers are outcome-specific selected participants, not all creators or all scheduled windows. Absence of observed switching does not establish structural nonpositivity. Noncreation includes heterogeneous activities and is not a standardised control; the 60- and 180-minute outcomes are distinct fixed-time associations, not a trajectory contrast. Preserve the two-test Holm family if one secondary model is unavailable. H2 does not require an observed first-follow-up valence; H3 requires baseline desired arousal and first-follow-up arousal. Later creation can influence H2 and is not conditioned upon in the primary H2 model.
\subsection{Missingness, selection and timing sensitivity}
Enumerate all scheduled and active prompt windows, cancelled prompts after withdrawal, timely baseline responses, ongoing exclusions, known exposures, valid outcomes and complete switchers. Missing exposure and outcome require separate sensitivity analysis; no missing exposure may be recoded as zero. For H1, evaluate sequential inverse-probability weights for exposure and outcome availability among valid baselines (caps 10 and 20), bounded deterministic outcome shifts among known exposures (additive shifts -2 to 2 and creation-specific shifts -1 to 1), and stochastic exposure scenarios among valid outcomes (log-odds shifts -1, 0, 1). Scenario ranges are not confidence intervals. The separate analyses do not identify jointly missing values. A supplementary joint scenario analysis uses a working exposure probability model including baseline covariates and observed outcome information where available, with an explicit outcome-availability indicator. It varies missing-exposure log odds by -1, 0 and 1, and shifts the working conditional mean of missing creation-window outcomes by -1, 0 and 1 raw points relative to the corresponding unshifted prediction. Observed responses remain unchanged. Missing outcomes are bounded to 0--10; participant offsets are estimated only for participants with at least one complete known window. The nine crossed scenarios report medians, simulation quantiles and failed fits, not confidence intervals. The analysis excludes invalid baselines and people without a complete known window, so it does not identify a full-cohort estimand. Exposure models, conditional-mean extrapolation and shift values require pilot-informed justification. Bootstrap uncertainty and independent calibration remain pending. Evaluate overlap, cluster influence and a prespecified narrower timing tolerance (baseline 0--5 minutes, exposure and first follow-up 60--65 minutes, later follow-up 180--185 minutes). The complete-case estimand may differ from the full-window target. Unmeasured time-varying confounding, changes in affect that precede creation within a window, anticipation and reporting reactivity prevent causal attribution.
\subsection{Feasibility and simulation design}
Conduct two iterative rounds of cognitive interviews (5--8 adults per round) and a separate 15--25-person feasibility pilot to assess prompt burden, frequency of creation windows, switching, timing compliance, score variation and missingness. Provisional review triggers include active baseline completion below 70 percent, exposure ascertainment below 80 percent of valid baselines, H1 completeness below 60 percent of valid baselines, median known-exposure creation prevalence below 10 percent, or complete switchers below 60 percent of enrolled participants. These are design-review triggers, not participant exclusion rules. Use participant-level uncertainty for frequency estimates and do not select hypotheses by pilot significance. Exclude pilot data from confirmation. Simulate 80--320 enrolled participants, 21 planned windows, creation prevalence .10--.40, heterogeneous individual propensities, correlated affect and 15--45\% missingness, including informative missingness. Examine effects of .0, .2, .4 and .6 valence points, coverage, bias, power and probability of sufficient switchers. Justify a smallest effect before final sample selection. The implemented 44-cell initial screen varies recruitment, creation prevalence, latent slopes and stage-specific missingness; it uses the production estimator, random propensities and slopes, serially correlated residuals and a rounded bounded outcome. Reference coefficients are estimated separately from large complete-data samples. Rejection of zero under unmeasured confounding is not a type-I error estimate for a causal null. Conditional rejection must be accompanied by the fraction of estimable datasets. The initial CI screen completed 8,800 synthetic replications without numerical failure. Under 30 percent expected stage loss, MAR and latent contrast 0.4, rejection rates for 160 enrolled participants were 83.5 and 99 percent at creation prevalence 0.10 and 0.25, respectively. Mean complete switcher counts were 88.3 and 132.38. Under unmeasured confounding with zero latent effect, rejection of zero was 98.5 percent, consistent with a nonzero reference associational coefficient. These preliminary results are not empirical findings or a final sample-size recommendation. A prespecified extension evaluates creation prevalence of 5, 10 and 25 percent at 160 and 320 enrolled participants, with 15 or 45 percent final-day withdrawal, additional ongoing-creation exclusions and timing loss. It reports unconditional rejection over all simulated datasets, the lower fifth percentile of usable switcher counts and coverage relative to complete eligible-window reference coefficients. The withdrawal model is informative and not necessarily MAR relative to observed data. In 24,000 synthetic replications without numerical failure, unconditional rejection at 45 percent final-day withdrawal and latent contrast 0.4 was 37.9, 56.2 and 88.7 percent for 160 enrolled participants at 5, 10 and 25 percent prevalence, respectively, versus 61.4, 88.7 and 99.5 percent for 320. Recruitment cannot be fixed without pilot-informed frequency and a justified smallest effect.

\subsection{Secondary outcome calibration}
The full secondary simulation comprises 72 scenarios crossing $N=160$ and 320, creation prevalence .05, .10 and .25, four pairs of conditional-mean targets $(0,0)$, $(.4,0)$, $(0,-.2)$ and $(.4,-.2)$, and three response-stage missingness mechanisms. Each scenario contains 2,000 independent synthetic datasets, yielding 144,000 datasets and 288,000 endpoint-specific fit attempts. All fits succeeded. These targets are hypothetical raw-score associations, not validated smallest effects. The generator includes 45\% final-day withdrawal, ongoing-creation exclusions and timing loss in every scenario. The MCAR/MAR/MNAR labels refer to the varied response-stage mechanism, not the entire observation process; withdrawal can be informative relative to available data. H2 and H3 have distinct outcome masks and use the fixed Holm family.

Across null and partial-null cells, maximum false-family rejection is .040 under MCAR, .050 under MAR and .405 under joint MNAR (81/200 at $N=1000$, prevalence .10, global null; Monte Carlo standard error .0347). Under joint MNAR, marginal coverage ranges from .400 to .870 for H2 and .670 to .945 for H3; bias ranges from -.197 to -.134 and -.073 to -.036 raw points, respectively. Larger samples reduce random error but cannot remove the selection bias.

For a separate missing-outcome sensitivity, restrict the target to timely valid baselines with known exposure and, for H3, known initial desired arousal. Determine switchers from this fixed design before outcome selection. Let $w$ be the exposure column of the linear coefficient operator after participant demeaning, so the coefficient for a complete response vector is $w^\top y$. If $O$ is the observed outcome set and $M$ its complement, the sharp finite-design bounds over the permitted outcome range $[0,U_j]$ are
\[
 L=\sum_{j\in O}w_jy_j+\sum_{j\in M}\min(0,w_jU_j),
 \quad
 H=\sum_{j\in O}w_jy_j+\sum_{j\in M}\max(0,w_jU_j).
\]
Here $U_j=10$ for valence and $U_j=\max(A_{\mathrm{desired},0},10-A_{\mathrm{desired},0})$ for absolute goal deviation. The bounds require a full-rank known-exposure switcher design and are exact for the finite-sample coefficient with arbitrary missing outcomes in those ranges. They are neither sampling confidence intervals nor bounds for participants with unknown exposure or missing baselines. Under all 72 extended scenarios both endpoint bounds included zero in every replicate, including the joint alternatives. Across joint-MNAR cells, mean widths are 3.32--3.74 points for H2 and 2.02--2.22 for H3. This illustrates how much outcome uncertainty can remain despite large samples; it does not demonstrate a zero population association. The production analysis now reports these bounds separately from complete-case inference and the H1 joint scenario sensitivity. A full larger-replication calibration, pilot-informed selection assumptions and uncertainty analysis remain necessary before a recruitment decision.

\section{Ethics and reproducibility}
Consent, ethics approval, approved recruitment and data storage precede the pilot. Do not collect songs, prompts, diagnoses or private narratives. The study is not treatment or monitored crisis support. Use pseudonymous identifiers and approved institutional storage; never commit participant-level records to Git. Before the pilot, verify informed consent, the approved contact and distress procedure, notification cancellation and the separation of contact information and study identifiers. Participants may skip prompts or discontinue without penalty; no explanation or personal narrative is required. Any request to erase previously collected records must be resolved under the approved retention policy before producing an analysis export. Staff should provide the approved support information when requested rather than interpreting a rating as a clinical alert. The collection platform, responsible controller, retention period and permissions remain subject to institutional approval. Freeze timing, instruments, sample target and analysis before confirmatory recruitment.
\section{Planned empirical reporting}
No participants have been recruited and no empirical outcomes are available. Report all prespecified results and deviations regardless of direction. Provide participant flow and window denominators separately, exposure support by endpoint, response delays and outcome distributions, H1 estimate and interval, the two adjusted secondary tests, and prespecified sensitivity comparisons. Report unavailable models and reasons. A participant with no usable windows remains in the enrolment denominator where permitted by the approved retention policy.
\section{Discussion}
\subsection{Scientific contribution and competing explanations}
Prescheduled observation windows provide a natural non-creation comparison without assuming that creation occurs at random. The approach captures everyday short-horizon affective associations, but is vulnerable to selective participation, time-varying causes of creation and incomplete exposure reports. Results must not be interpreted as clinical benefit, physiological arousal or evidence of neural mechanisms. The literature search was targeted rather than systematic. A positive H1 with weak or null H2 would be consistent with a short-horizon association, but would not identify decay of an effect: the endpoint-specific samples and intervening activities differ. A null H1 with lower goal deviation in H3 could be consistent with regulation directed at activation rather than pleasantness; it could also reflect baseline goal selection, measurement error or incomplete observation. A negative H1 does not establish harm from creation. Interpret all combinations against intervals, response support and missingness diagnostics, rather than privileging significant outcomes.

\subsection{Implications of the synthetic calibrations}
The simulations distinguish three constraints on scientific informativeness: the availability of within-person exposure contrasts, the precision of the estimated association and the validity of inference under selective observation. Increasing enrolment can improve the first two without resolving the third. For example, the expanded screen exhibits substantially elevated false-family rejection under joint MNAR despite a large nominal sample (see the expanded calibration section). This finding is conditional on the specified generator; it is not an estimate of the prevalence or severity of MNAR in the planned study. Adjustment for multiplicity controls the statistical testing procedure under suitable assumptions, but it does not repair selection bias in an endpoint-specific coefficient.

Interpret a future complete-case estimate alongside the endpoint-specific exposure support and missingness diagnostics. Concordance between the primary estimate and the sensitivity scenarios would indicate stability over those particular assumptions, not identification of the association under all missingness mechanisms. Discordance in sign or magnitude should be reported as substantive sensitivity rather than resolved by selecting the most favourable result. The finite-design bounds for H2 and H3 complement rather than replace the sampling intervals. When such bounds include zero, the direction of the fixed-design association is not determined by the observed outcomes and the scale restrictions alone. Bounds excluding zero would still apply only to the known-exposure switcher design and would not establish a population or causal conclusion.

The present calibrations cover selected data-generating mechanisms, not all plausible patterns of dependence. The secondary generators use bounded conditional means and goal deviations with restricted support; empirical deviations could occupy a wider range. The simulations cannot validate the original EMA items, establish the psychological relevance of a given raw-score contrast or resolve the dependence of reporting on unobserved affect. The local secondary and expanded archives have undergone structural verification, but independent full numerical reproduction remains outstanding. The pilot should inform the frequency of usable contrasts, timing and response loss; its observations cannot determine the unobserved component of an MNAR mechanism.

\subsection{Limitations and subsequent research}
The availability band excludes some daily contexts, and one window per day cannot capture all creation episodes or estimate continuous-time affective inertia. The first-hour binary exposure combines brief and prolonged activities, different interfaces and creative goals. Even a temporally preceding baseline may be influenced by anticipation. Follow-up ratings at submission may be influenced by activity after the nominal horizon. Within-person adjustment and the timing sensitivity do not solve these problems.

The design also excludes ongoing creation at baseline. The target is consequently an association among eligible observed switchers, not the emotional experience of all creators or all stages of creation. Voluntary participation and the requirement to respond within a daily availability band may favour participants with more flexible schedules or particular patterns of technology use. Differences between eligible observed switchers and other enrolled participants should be described using approved available information, without implying that weighting by observed variables can reconstruct the experience of people with no usable windows. The absence of an assigned comparison activity also limits interpretation of noncreation windows, which can include listening, rest or unrelated activities.

The original single-item ratings capture reported momentary experience. Their response scales may be interpreted differently across people and occasions. Participant fixed effects do not eliminate changing response standards, and a narrow confidence interval cannot establish construct validity. The H3 endpoint measures distance from the initial goal, which may change during creative activity; it does not capture successful regulation across all possible goals. These limitations should inform the interpretation of null as well as positive findings.

The enrolment timezone remains fixed across the 21-day schedule. Travel may alter the relationship between clock time and daily context, even when elapsed times are correctly computed in UTC. This limitation should be documented without collecting location history. No retrospective change to the schedule or timezone is permitted.

Subsequent research could distinguish active prompting, selection and editing, and investigate whether candidate mechanisms such as agency or frustration precede affective changes. These extensions would require separate operational definitions and measurement evidence. A controlled study could manipulate a defined affordance or randomise an encouragement, with independently specified causal estimands, rather than treating the present observational coefficient as an intervention effect. An alternative sampling design with denser assessments would require renewed feasibility and burden evaluation before investigating emotional trajectories.

\section{Conclusion}
P07 provides a prospective protocol for estimating short-horizon within-person associations of natural AI-assisted music creation with subjective affect. Its value lies in explicit observation windows, separation of valence from goal-relative activation, transparent exposure support and calibrated reporting of missingness and uncertainty. Feasibility, response-process evidence, a justified effect target and institutional approval remain prerequisites to confirmatory data collection.
