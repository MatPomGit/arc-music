\title{Emotional Dynamics of AI-Assisted Music Creation in Everyday Life: A Prospective Ecological Momentary Assessment Protocol}
\author{Mateusz Pomianek}
\date{Prospective protocol, version 0.10.0, 27 November 2026}
\begin{document}
\maketitle
\begin{abstract}
AI-assisted music creation may coincide with changes in everyday affect, but event-triggered assessments alone cannot distinguish creation-related trajectories from ordinary affective fluctuations. We propose a 21-day observational ecological momentary assessment (EMA) study with independently scheduled observation windows. Current valence and arousal are assessed at baseline and approximately one and three hours later. Natural creation during the first hour is contrasted with windows without creation within the same person, adjusting for baseline affect, intention, study day and local time. The primary endpoint is valence at the first follow-up. Secondary outcomes concern later valence and proximity to the participant's initial arousal goal. Exposure is self-selected; the contrast is associative rather than causal. Separate synthetic calibrations of the primary (32,800 datasets) and secondary (144,000 datasets) endpoints illustrate dependence on creation frequency and usable within-person contrasts. In the secondary screen, maximum false-family rejection was .0445 and .0425 under the MCAR and MAR response-stage scenarios, versus .2905 under MNAR. No recruitment target is approved and no participant data or empirical results are available.
\end{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} affect dynamics; valence; arousal; ecological momentary assessment; generative AI; music creation; within-person association.
\section{Introduction}
The circumplex account distinguishes affective pleasantness from activation \citep{russell1980}. A rise in activation is not intrinsically beneficial or harmful; its meaning depends on context and desired affect. Emotion regulation includes attempts to influence emotional experience, rather than uniformly maximizing positive affect \citep{gross2015}. Everyday music research has used experience sampling to characterize situational variation in music-evoked emotion and listening motives \citep{juslin2008,randall2017}. These studies address listening rather than generative creation. AI songwriting adds iterative generation, selection and editing; observations of competition teams establish the relevance of creative iteration, not its effect on mood \citep{huang2020}.
\par Affective persistence and variability are distinguishable constructs \citep{kuppens2010,houben2015}. Sparse, irregularly spaced reports do not identify continuous-time emotional inertia without stronger assumptions. We therefore focus on fixed observation horizons rather than interpreting a raw autocorrelation as regulatory capacity. EMA reduces dependence on distant recall \citep{shiffman2008}; separation of within-person and between-person variation is essential \citep{curran2011}.
\subsection{From music engagement to everyday creative decisions}
AI-assisted creation may involve anticipation, effort, uncertainty, repeated revision and evaluation of the resulting music. These operations are distinct from listening to an existing piece. A person may initiate creation when already distressed, when expecting enjoyment or when an opportunity becomes available. The same apparent improvement in valence may therefore reflect selection into the activity, recovery from an earlier state, concurrent activities or consequences of creation. The design is intended to characterise temporally ordered associations, not to decide among these explanations on its own.

Valence and arousal are complementary dimensions of subjective affect. A creator may value an activating experience or seek calm; neither low nor high activation is universally preferable. H3 concerns correspondence with a previously stated activation goal, which is not identical to emotional clarity, psychological ownership or goal attainment in a broader sense. This distinction prevents interpreting all movement towards a pleasant state as successful regulation. The goal can change within a session; the endpoint specifically uses the initial goal and cannot distinguish goal revision from failure to reach it.

\subsection{Why scheduled observation windows?}
A meta-analysis of 496 EMA samples from 477 articles reported mean compliance of approximately 79\% across heterogeneous designs \citep{wrzus2023}. This is a contextual benchmark, not an expected rate for P07 or evidence that three daily assessments will be feasible. Event-contingent reports of successful creation alone omit ordinary noncreation moments, abandoned attempts and missed opportunities. Scheduling windows independently of creation provides a defined denominator but does not randomise exposure or ensure that actual responses represent all scheduled moments.

The review by \citet{stone2023} highlights unresolved issues in momentary self-report, including interpretation, comparison standards, participant training, selection, missingness and reliability. For P07, this motivates separate recording of prompt, submission and exposure times and explicit response-process checks. Ratings refer to the current experience at actual submission, not the remembered state at the scheduled prompt. Consequently, delaying submission can change the moment sampled even when the answer falls within the accepted interval. Narrow timing analyses assess sensitivity to this choice but may introduce a different selection process.

\section{Research question and hypotheses}
Is first-follow-up valence associated with naturally occurring AI-assisted creation during the prescheduled first hour among participants with complete windows of both exposure types?
\begin{description}
\item[P07-H1 (primary)] First-follow-up valence is higher in creation windows than non-creation windows, conditional on baseline valence, arousal, intention, time of day and study day. A two-sided test permits a contrary result.
\item[P07-H2 (secondary)] Estimate the association of first-hour creation with valence at the three-hour follow-up, using the same baseline covariates. This is not a test of persistence of an isolated intervention because later activities may differ.
\item[P07-H3 (secondary)] First-follow-up absolute deviation from the baseline desired arousal level is lower in creation windows, adjusting for initial deviation and the primary baseline covariates. No direction is assumed for arousal itself.
\end{description}
Exploratory analyses describe valence--arousal trajectories, listening-only windows, duration, later creation and heterogeneity. A precise null or reversed H1 constrains the directional hypothesis. The study is distinct from P01's assigned-task contrasts, P03's recalled motives, P06's functional-interference endpoint and P08's manipulation of creative agency. If participants are shared, disclose overlap and assess publication redundancy before submission.
\section{Method}
\subsection{Participants and observation design}
Recruit consenting Polish-speaking adult AI music creators who report recent routine use. Do not prescribe creation, paid platform use or emotional material. During 21 days, issue one prescheduled prompt per day at a randomly selected minute within a participant-defined available time band, leaving at least 210 minutes (15 minutes for baseline submission and 195 minutes for follow-up). Freeze the schedule independently of affect and actual creation; record every scheduled window, including missed prompts. A frozen enrollment-derived schedule defines all 21 planned local dates and immutable window identifiers, using a fixed participant-defined availability band and IANA time zone. The analysis reconciles responses against this schedule and preserves windows without response records. An offset-aware cessation log separates cancelled prompts at or after withdrawal from missed active prompts. Partial pre-withdrawal responses in an initiated window remain subject to approved retention rules; responses at or after cessation are invalid. Baseline is valid within 15 minutes of the prompt. Follow-up prompts are scheduled at 60 and 180 minutes from actual baseline submission; accept 60--75 and 180--195 minutes. Do not open follow-ups early. Participants may skip any response. The maximum burden is 21 daily windows and 63 scheduled assessment opportunities conditional on timely baseline response and continued participation. Follow-up prompts depend on baseline submission; distinguish planned opportunities from actually issued prompts. Figure~\ref{fig:ema-window} summarises the timing contract.

\subsection{Exposure and outcome measurement}
\par The exposure is any active AI-assisted music creation (prompting, generating, choosing or editing outputs as part of a creative task) during the first 60 minutes after baseline, regardless of completion. Listening alone is not creation. Collect actual first-hour exposure at the first follow-up or a separate exposure check, with its own timestamp; a later recollection is exploratory only. Record whether creation was already ongoing at baseline, and exclude ongoing windows from the primary comparison. Record pre-window intention, baseline context, other music activity, and later creation separately. Uncertain exposure is missing, never non-creation. Do not use post-baseline activities as primary adjustment covariates.
\par Valence (0 very unpleasant, 10 very pleasant), arousal (0 very low activation, 10 very high activation) and desired arousal (same activation anchors) are proposed original single-item ratings, not validated clinical scales. The current state, not the emotional content of the song, is rated. Desired arousal is elicited before exposure. Assess interpretation through cognitive interviews and feasibility pilot. No proprietary questionnaire items are included. Valence and arousal are bounded ordinal responses treated as numerical scores for the prespecified linear projection. This is a modelling convention, not a demonstration of equal perceptual intervals. Assess endpoint use and within-person variation in the pilot; no post hoc exclusion based on restricted affect variation.

\section{Statistical analysis}
\subsection{Target association and interpretation}
Let $C_{it}$ indicate creation in the first hour, $V_{it,0}$ and $A_{it,0}$ denote baseline affect and $I_{it,0}$ pre-window intention (0--10). For the primary outcome $V_{it,60}$ use
\[
V_{it,60}=\alpha_i+\beta C_{it}+\gamma V_{it,0}+
\delta A_{it,0}+\theta I_{it,0}+\eta d_{it}+\lambda h_{it}+\epsilon_{it},
\]
where $d$ is study day and $h$ is local baseline hour in decimal hours. Restrict the initial analysis to valid complete windows of participants with both exposure conditions represented, so $\beta$ describes the adjusted within-person linear projection in observed switchers. The target is not an average treatment effect or all creators. Fixed effects remove stable additive person-level differences, not unmeasured time-varying influences. The pooled coefficient weights participants according to their residualised exposure information, rather than assigning equal weight to each participant-specific association. With heterogeneous slopes, interpret it as a within-person projection in the retained sample. Do not equate it with the average individual slope. A coefficient of .4 denotes an adjusted .4-point difference on the rating scale, not a standardised effect, a clinical threshold or a change score. The pilot will assess response interpretation and support; the smallest meaningful association must be justified independently of significance. Use participant fixed effects, CR1 cluster standard errors with participant intercepts counted in degrees of freedom, and $t$ inference with $G-1$ degrees of freedom. Report coefficient and 95\% interval; require at least ten switcher clusters and full design rank as computational minima, not adequate sample size. H2 substitutes three-hour valence; H3 substitutes absolute arousal-goal deviation and includes baseline deviation. Holm adjustment covers H2 and H3; each uses its outcome-specific complete switcher subset. Report counts of complete windows and switcher participants, and compare the common complete subset descriptively. For each endpoint report all enrolled participants as no complete known window, noncreation only, creation only, or both, and compare this with support among valid baselines with known exposure. Observed switchers are outcome-specific selected participants, not all creators or all scheduled windows. Absence of observed switching does not establish structural nonpositivity. Noncreation includes heterogeneous activities and is not a standardized control; the 60- and 180-minute outcomes are distinct fixed-time associations, not a trajectory contrast. Preserve the two-test Holm family if one secondary model is unavailable. H2 does not require an observed first-follow-up valence; H3 requires baseline desired arousal and first-follow-up arousal. Later creation can influence H2 and is not conditioned upon in the primary H2 model.
\subsection{Missingness, selection and timing sensitivity}
Enumerate all scheduled and active prompt windows, cancelled prompts after withdrawal, timely baseline responses, ongoing exclusions, known exposures, valid outcomes and complete switchers. Missing exposure and outcome require separate sensitivity analysis; no missing exposure may be recoded as zero. For H1, evaluate sequential inverse-probability weights for exposure and outcome availability among valid baselines (caps 10 and 20), bounded deterministic outcome shifts among known exposures (additive shifts -2 to 2 and creation-specific shifts -1 to 1), and stochastic exposure scenarios among valid outcomes (log-odds shifts -1, 0, 1). Scenario ranges are not confidence intervals. The separate analyses do not identify jointly missing values. A supplementary joint scenario analysis uses a working exposure probability model including baseline covariates and observed outcome information where available, with an explicit outcome-availability indicator. It varies missing-exposure log odds by -1, 0 and 1, and shifts the working conditional mean of missing creation-window outcomes by -1, 0 and 1 raw points relative to the corresponding unshifted prediction. Observed responses remain unchanged. Missing outcomes are bounded to 0--10; participant offsets are estimated only for participants with at least one complete known window. The nine crossed scenarios report medians, simulation quantiles and failed fits, not confidence intervals. The analysis excludes invalid baselines and people without a complete known window, so it does not identify a full-cohort estimand. Exposure models, conditional-mean extrapolation and shift values require pilot-informed justification. Bootstrap uncertainty and independent calibration remain pending. Evaluate overlap, cluster influence and a prespecified narrower timing tolerance (baseline 0--5 minutes, exposure and first follow-up 60--65 minutes, later follow-up 180--185 minutes). The complete-case estimand may differ from the full-window target. Unmeasured time-varying confounding, changes in affect that precede creation within a window, anticipation and reporting reactivity prevent causal attribution.
\subsection{Feasibility and simulation design}
Conduct two iterative rounds of cognitive interviews (5--8 adults per round) and a separate 15--25-person feasibility pilot to assess prompt burden, frequency of creation windows, switching, timing compliance, score variation and missingness. Provisional review triggers include active baseline completion below 70 percent, exposure ascertainment below 80 percent of valid baselines, H1 completeness below 60 percent of valid baselines, median known-exposure creation prevalence below 10 percent, or complete switchers below 60 percent of enrollees. These are design-review triggers, not participant exclusion rules. Use participant-level uncertainty for frequency estimates and do not select hypotheses by pilot significance. Exclude pilot data from confirmation. Simulate 80--320 enrolled participants, 21 planned windows, creation prevalence .10--.40, heterogeneous individual propensities, correlated affect and 15--45\% missingness, including informative missingness. Examine effects of .0, .2, .4 and .6 valence points, coverage, bias, power and probability of sufficient switchers. Justify a smallest effect before final sample selection. The implemented 44-cell initial screen varies recruitment, creation prevalence, latent slopes and stage-specific missingness; it uses the production estimator, random propensities and slopes, serially correlated residuals and a rounded bounded outcome. Reference coefficients are estimated separately from large complete-data samples. Rejection of zero under unmeasured confounding is not a type-I error estimate for a causal null. Conditional rejection must be accompanied by the fraction of estimable datasets. The initial CI screen completed 8,800 synthetic replications without numerical failure. Under 30 percent expected stage loss, MAR and latent contrast 0.4, rejection rates for 160 enrollees were 83.5 and 99 percent at creation prevalence 0.10 and 0.25, respectively. Mean complete switcher counts were 88.3 and 132.38. Under unmeasured confounding with zero latent effect, rejection of zero was 98.5 percent, consistent with a nonzero reference associational coefficient. These preliminary results are not empirical findings or a final sample-size recommendation. A prespecified extension evaluates creation prevalence of 5, 10 and 25 percent at 160 and 320 enrollees, with 15 or 45 percent final-day withdrawal, additional ongoing-creation exclusions and timing loss. It reports unconditional rejection over all simulated datasets, the lower fifth percentile of usable switcher counts and coverage relative to complete eligible-window reference coefficients. The withdrawal model is informative and not necessarily MAR relative to observed data. In 24,000 synthetic replications without numerical failure, unconditional rejection at 45 percent final-day withdrawal and latent contrast 0.4 was 37.9, 56.2 and 88.7 percent for 160 enrollees at 5, 10 and 25 percent prevalence, respectively, versus 61.4, 88.7 and 99.5 percent for 320. Recruitment cannot be fixed without pilot-informed frequency and a justified smallest effect.

\subsection{Secondary outcome calibration}
The full secondary simulation comprises 72 scenarios crossing $N=160$ and 320, creation prevalence .05, .10 and .25, four pairs of conditional-mean targets $(0,0)$, $(.4,0)$, $(0,-.2)$ and $(.4,-.2)$, and three response-stage missingness mechanisms. Each scenario contains 2,000 independent synthetic datasets, yielding 144,000 datasets and 288,000 endpoint-specific fit attempts. All fits succeeded. These targets are hypothetical raw-score associations, not validated smallest effects. The generator includes 45\% final-day withdrawal, ongoing-creation exclusions and timing loss in every scenario. The MCAR/MAR/MNAR labels refer to the varied response-stage mechanism, not the entire observation process; withdrawal can be informative relative to available data. H2 and H3 have distinct outcome masks and use the fixed Holm family.

\section{Ethics and reproducibility}
Consent, ethics approval, approved recruitment and data storage precede the pilot. Do not collect songs, prompts, diagnoses or private narratives. The study is not treatment or monitored crisis support. Use pseudonymous identifiers and approved institutional storage; never commit participant-level records to Git. Freeze timing, instruments, sample target and analysis before confirmatory recruitment.
\section{Planned empirical reporting}
No participants have been recruited and no empirical outcomes are available. Report all prespecified results and deviations regardless of direction. Provide participant flow and window denominators separately, exposure support by endpoint, response delays and outcome distributions, H1 estimate and interval, the two adjusted secondary tests, and prespecified sensitivity comparisons. Report unavailable models and reasons. A participant with no usable windows remains in the enrollment denominator where permitted by the approved retention policy.
\section{Discussion}
\subsection{Scientific contribution and competing explanations}
Prescheduled observation windows provide a natural non-creation comparison without assuming that creation occurs at random. The approach captures everyday short-horizon affective associations, but is vulnerable to selective participation, time-varying causes of creation and incomplete exposure reports. Results must not be interpreted as clinical benefit, physiological arousal or evidence of neural mechanisms. The initial bibliography is targeted, not systematic. A positive H1 with weak or null H2 would be consistent with a short-horizon association, but would not identify decay of an effect: the endpoint-specific samples and intervening activities differ. A null H1 with lower goal deviation in H3 could be consistent with regulation directed at activation rather than pleasantness; it could also reflect baseline goal selection, measurement error or incomplete observation. A negative H1 does not establish harm from creation. Interpret all combinations against intervals, response support and missingness diagnostics, rather than privileging significant outcomes.

\subsection{Limitations and subsequent research}
The availability band excludes some daily contexts, and one window per day cannot capture all creation episodes or estimate continuous-time affective inertia. The first-hour binary exposure combines brief and prolonged activities, different interfaces and creative goals. Even a temporally preceding baseline may be influenced by anticipation. Follow-up ratings at submission may be influenced by activity after the nominal horizon. Within-person adjustment and the timing sensitivity do not solve these problems. Later experiments could manipulate a defined affordance or randomise an encouragement, with independently specified causal estimands, rather than interpreting the current coefficient as an intervention effect.
\paragraph{Time-zone policy.} Compute baseline local hour by converting the recorded instant to the frozen enrollment IANA timezone; response offsets are serialization details. Calculate elapsed minutes in UTC. The schedule remains in the enrollment timezone throughout the 21-day period, including travel and daylight-saving transitions. Travel may alter the relationship between clock time and daily context; document this limitation without collecting location history. No retrospective change of schedule or timezone is permitted.

\section{Conclusion}
P07 provides a prospective protocol for estimating short-horizon within-person associations of natural AI-assisted music creation with subjective affect. Its value lies in explicit observation windows, separation of valence from goal-relative activation, transparent exposure support and calibrated reporting of missingness and uncertainty. Feasibility, response-process evidence, a justified effect target and institutional approval remain prerequisites to confirmatory data collection.
