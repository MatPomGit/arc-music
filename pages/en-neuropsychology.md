# Music, the brain and emotions

[PL](neuropsychology.md) · [EN](en-neuropsychology.md)

Music connects perception, memory, prediction, emotion and action. AIM-ER (AI-assisted Music for Emotion Regulation) examines the transition from personal experience to a musical piece created with generative AI. We examine whether giving emotions a musical form changes how they are experienced and understood.

## From sound to meaning

The auditory system analyses frequency, temporal structure and timbre. Recognising melody and rhythm involves integrating information over time and comparing it with previous experience. Anticipating the next note or the resolution of harmonic tension can shape emotional responses to a piece.

Music can evoke emotions through multiple mechanisms, including episodic memory, musical expectancy, imagery and conditioning. The same sound may therefore elicit different reactions in different people ([Juslin and Västfjäll, 2008](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X08005293)).

## Perception, emotion and reward

Musical pleasure involves interactions between the auditory cortex and reward circuitry, including the nucleus accumbens ([Salimpoor et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1231059)). Dopamine release has been observed during intensely pleasurable music listening ([Salimpoor et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn.2726)).

Musical emotions engage distributed networks, including the amygdala, insula, prefrontal cortex and memory-related structures. No single region accounts for all music-evoked emotions ([Koelsch, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn3666)).

## Autobiographical memory and identity

Familiar music can spontaneously evoke personal memories. Research implicates the medial prefrontal cortex in processing music associated with autobiographical experience ([Janata, 2009](https://doi.org/10.1093/cercor/bhp008)).

A piece created from one's own story may become a new memory cue. Listening could support reflection but could also reactivate distressing memories. The direction of this response may depend on context, personal meaning and individual emotion regulation strategies.

## Affect labelling and cognitive regulation

Writing lyrics requires selecting words, organising content and making sense of experiences. An experiment on affect labelling found reduced amygdala reactivity ([Lieberman et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01916.x)). This does not establish an identical effect during autobiographical songwriting.

The process model of emotion regulation distinguishes attentional deployment, cognitive change and response modulation ([Gross, 2015](https://doi.org/10.1080/1047840X.2014.940781)). Different stages of AIM-ER (AI-assisted Music for Emotion Regulation) may engage distinct strategies, which we intend to examine separately.

## Agency, authorship and AI-assisted creation

A music generator lowers the technical barriers to composition. Users can formulate prompts, select versions, revise lyrics and evaluate successive outputs. Perceived control over the process need not coincide with perceived authorship of the resulting piece.

AIM-ER (AI-assisted Music for Emotion Regulation) hypothesises that deliberate selection and iteration may facilitate agency and reappraisal. A mismatch between generated music and the creator's intention may instead cause frustration or weaken identification with the piece.

## Benefits and potential difficulties

A meta-analysis of music engagement interventions reported a moderate average effect on emotion regulation ([Peters et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1177/03057356231212362)). Evidence for AI-based music interventions remains of very low certainty ([Jin, 2026](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2026.1967909)).

Immediate relief may coexist with avoidance, rumination or increased arousal. Both immediate responses and subsequent functioning matter. Neural mechanisms specific to AI-assisted autobiographical music creation have not been established. Music creation is not a substitute for psychotherapy.

## How can these mechanisms be studied?

Our initial studies are planned to use interviews and surveys concerning creative motives, emotion regulation, alexithymia, rumination, agency and authorship. Subsequent experiments could compare lyric writing, listening to existing music and creating a personal piece with AI.

Distinguishing valence from arousal helps capture changes obscured by a single mood scale. Future self-report measures could be supplemented with psychophysiological indicators such as electrodermal activity and heart rate. These indicators do not uniquely identify particular emotions.
