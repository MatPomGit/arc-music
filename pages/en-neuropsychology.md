# Music, the brain and emotions

[PL](neuropsychology.md) · [EN](en-neuropsychology.md)

## How does music affect the brain?

Music listening engages neural networks involved in auditory perception, emotion, memory and reward. Musical pleasure is associated with reward-system activity; dopamine release has been observed during listening to intensely pleasurable music ([Koelsch, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn3666); [Salimpoor et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn.2726)).

## From experience to music

Writing lyrics allows experiences to be put into words. Studies of affect labelling suggest that naming emotions can alter amygdala reactivity ([Lieberman et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01916.x)). Familiar music may evoke autobiographical memories, accompanied by activity in regions including the medial prefrontal cortex ([Janata, 2009](https://doi.org/10.1093/cercor/bhp008)).

## AI-assisted emotion regulation

Music generators allow people to give their words a musical form without training in composition. Selecting sounds, editing and listening to a track may facilitate reappraisal, agency and psychological distancing. These are hypotheses of the AIM-ER framework. A meta-analysis of music listening, performance and creation found a favourable average effect on emotion regulation ([Peters et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1177/03057356231212362)).

Evidence from AI-based music interventions remains of very low certainty ([Jin, 2026](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2026.1967909)). Neural mechanisms specific to creating autobiographical music with AI have not been established. Immediate relief may coexist with rumination or avoidance. Music creation is not a substitute for psychotherapy.
