# Music, the brain and emotions

[PL](neuropsychology.md) · [EN](en-neuropsychology.md)

Music connects perception, memory, emotion and action. Its psychological significance emerges from the interaction of sound, personal history and meaning. These processes form a foundation for studying emotion regulation and AI-assisted creativity.

<div class="neuro-evidence" aria-label="Selected research findings">

## Psychological and neural mechanisms

<div class="neuro-grid">
<section class="neuro-card" id="neuro-perception">
<span class="neuro-number" aria-hidden="true">01</span>
<h3>Perception and prediction</h3>
<p>Music perception engages recognition of sound patterns and prediction of what follows. Expectation, surprise and resolution of musical tension can contribute to emotional responses. Musical tension is not equivalent to psychological stress.</p>
<p class="neuro-sources"><a href="https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X08005293">Juslin &amp; Västfjäll, 2008</a> · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn3666">Koelsch, 2014</a></p>
</section>
<section class="neuro-card" id="neuro-reward">
<span class="neuro-number" aria-hidden="true">02</span>
<h3>Reward and motivation</h3>
<p>Musical reward involves distributed networks, including the nucleus accumbens. Dopamine release has been observed during intensely pleasurable listening. This establishes neither an identical effect during AI-assisted creation nor improved mental health.</p>
<p class="neuro-sources"><a href="https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1231059">Salimpoor et al., 2013</a> · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/nn.2726">Salimpoor et al., 2011</a></p>
</section>
<section class="neuro-card" id="neuro-memory">
<span class="neuro-number" aria-hidden="true">03</span>
<h3>Autobiographical memory and identity</h3>
<p>Music can cue personal memories together with associated emotions. Neuroimaging research implicates the medial prefrontal cortex. AIM-ER hypothesises that a personal piece may support reflection on one's history and its relationship to identity. Revisiting memories may also be distressing.</p>
<p class="neuro-sources"><a href="https://doi.org/10.1093/cercor/bhp008">Janata, 2009</a></p>
</section>
<section class="neuro-card" id="neuro-expression">
<span class="neuro-number" aria-hidden="true">04</span>
<h3>Expression and understanding emotions</h3>
<p>Putting experiences into words or music may facilitate differentiation and reflection. Affect-labelling research identifies changes in neural responses in specific laboratory tasks. Whether AI-assisted creation engages comparable mechanisms remains an empirical question.</p>
<p class="neuro-sources"><a href="https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01916.x">Lieberman et al., 2007</a> · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1080/1047840X.2014.940781">Gross, 2015</a></p>
</section>
<section class="neuro-card" id="neuro-stress">
<span class="neuro-number" aria-hidden="true">05</span>
<h3>Stress and tension regulation</h3>
<p>Subjective relief may involve changes in attention, interpretation or arousal. Meta-analyses of music interventions indicate favourable average effects but do not isolate the specific contribution of generative AI. Expression does not guarantee lasting relief; subsequent functioning also matters.</p>
<p class="neuro-sources"><a href="https://doi.org/10.1080/17437199.2019.1627897">de Witte et al., 2020</a> · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1080/1047840X.2014.940781">Gross, 2015</a> · <a href="https://doi.org/10.1177/03057356231212362">Peters et al., 2024</a></p>
</section>
<section class="neuro-card" id="neuro-agency">
<span class="neuro-number" aria-hidden="true">06</span>
<h3>Agency and creative autonomy</h3>
<p>A generator allows some compositional operations to be delegated while retaining opportunities for selection and editing. AIM-ER distinguishes available control, perceived influence over the outcome and psychological ownership. Their links with emotion regulation require testing.</p>
</section>
<section class="neuro-card" id="neuro-limits">
<span class="neuro-number" aria-hidden="true">07</span>
<h3>Individual differences and limitations</h3>
<p>The meaning of music depends on goals, context and individual experience. Repetition of an emotionally significant piece may support reflection or sustain rumination. Evidence on AI music interventions remains highly uncertain. AI-assisted creation is not an established therapeutic method.</p>
<p class="neuro-sources"><a href="https://doi.org/10.1177/03057356231212362">Peters et al., 2024</a> · <a href="https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2026.1967909">Jin, 2026</a></p>
</section>
<section class="neuro-card" id="neuro-measurement">
<span class="neuro-number" aria-hidden="true">08</span>
<h3>Measuring psychological processes</h3>
<p>Interviews and questionnaires examine meanings and reported experiences; repeated assessments examine variation over time. Experiments can evaluate specific creative conditions. Physiological measures might complement self-report in future work but do not uniquely identify particular emotions.</p>
</section>
</div>

## Evidence and open questions

Standardised stress-reduction effects were 0.545 for psychological and 0.380 for physiological outcomes (de Witte et al., 2020). Only two of eight emotion-regulation studies included creative activities. Jin (2026) rated evidence for AI music interventions very low across all outcome domains. These findings do not establish effects of autobiographical music creation with AI.
