# Research programme

[PL](studies.md) · [EN](en-studies.md)

## Creators' experiences

Individual interviews will examine motives for creating music, the circumstances in which tracks are produced and the meanings participants attribute to music in relation to their personal experiences

## Emotion regulation

P03 examines the association between expression motive and recalled ease of understanding emotions. Separate studies address alexithymia (P04), brooding and interference (P06), and everyday affect (P07). AIM-ER provides the theoretical basis for these distinct questions

## Agency and authorship

The programme examines personal contribution to music creation, perceived control over the finished work and changes in affect in everyday life

<p class="study-status">Study protocols are currently being developed.</p>

<h2 id="publication-title">Research projects</h2>
<p>Nine research projects develop AIM-ER (AI-assisted Music for Emotion Regulation), each addressing a distinct aspect of the psychology of AI-assisted music creation. Empirical studies are at the design stage; simulation findings describe the properties of statistical methods, not participants' experiences.</p>

<h3 id="msc-mus-p01-title">AIM-ER (AI-assisted Music for Emotion Regulation) model of emotion regulation</h3>
<p class="pub-question"><strong>Research question:</strong> Does emotion regulation depend on the fit between the creator’s goal and the affordances of the music generator?</p>
<div class="pub-body"><div><h4>Scope and methodology</h4><p>The model separates regulatory goals, creative operations (prompting, generation, selection and editing), psychological mechanisms, and immediate and delayed outcomes. Ten falsifiable hypotheses address distress reduction, personal resonance, authorship, agency, alexithymia, rumination, feedback, musical expertise, regulatory flexibility and novelty. A central prediction distinct from H1–H10 is tested in a proposed 2 × 2 experiment crossing goal (emotional articulation or reinterpretation) with interface affordance (preserving meaning or transforming perspective), with comparable duration and generator access.</p></div><div><h4>Scientific significance</h4><p>The primary experimental outcome is goal attainment, with emotional clarity and reinterpretation assessed separately. The framework predicts an advantage for matched conditions, not universal AI effectiveness. A precise null or reversed effect would weaken the prediction. The programme includes interviews, surveys, diary studies and experiments. None of the current P02–P09 companion protocols is a complete direct test of the central prediction or H1–H10; they provide partially related measures and foundations for further validation.</p></div></div>
<div class="pub-constructs"><p class="pub-definition"><strong>Constructs and conceptual meaning:</strong> Emotion regulation is goal-dependent shaping of emotional experience, not simply reduction of negative affect. Expression gives form to experience; reinterpretation changes its meaning. Agency, psychological ownership and personal resonance are distinct proposed AIM-ER mechanisms; available control is not equivalent to perceived control.</p><p class="pub-operationalisation"><strong>Operationalisation and indicators:</strong> Before a session, record the goal and current affect (distress, valence, arousal); during it, available and exercised creative operations; afterwards and at follow-up, attainment of the initial goal, emotional clarity, reinterpretation and functioning. The central experiment crosses articulation versus reinterpretation instructions with fidelity-preserving versus perspective-transforming interfaces. The common goal-attainment rating requires comparability across instructions.</p><p class="pub-interpretation"><strong>Interpretation of the outcome:</strong> The central prediction requires both positive goal-specific simple contrasts under a conjunction test; the mean matching contrast summarises magnitude separately. A result cannot show that AI is necessary for regulation or that a proposed mediator caused the effect. Mood, goal attainment and functioning are distinct outcomes.</p></div>
<div class="pub-parameters"><p class="pub-instruments"><strong>Measurement instruments:</strong> Candidates: PAQ-PL or TAS-20-POL-R (alexithymia), ERQ-S-PL (regulation), SUPIN/PANAS-PL (affect), HUMS (music use); original goal-attainment, agency and state ratings, operation logs.</p><p class="pub-sample"><strong>Sample size:</strong> Theoretical paper, no empirical sample. 2 × 2 experiment: conditional N=1000 is the first evaluated candidate exceeding 80% joint power for two .30 effects (85.76% and 81.77% across variance/retention profiles). Historical N=420 referred only to the mean contrast.</p><div class="pub-estimation"><p class="pub-estimate"><strong>Estimation results:</strong> <span class="pub-estimate-count">200,000 replications</span>. N=420, 105 per factorial cell; expected N=357 after 15% loss. At d=.30, analytical power 80.69%, simulated power 80.58%; type I error 5.08%, alternative 95% CI coverage 94.98%. Mean matching contrast only. The new conjunction screen uses 1,920,000 replications in 96 configurations: joint power at N=420 is 40.69% for symmetric .30 effects, and at N=1000 is 85.76% (MCSE 0.25 percentage points). With asymmetric .15/.45 effects, power at N=1000 is 45.98%. Effect sizes, measurement and recruitment remain unapproved.</p><p class="pub-estimate-note">Planning estimates and simulations, not participant results.</p></div></div>
<p class="pub-target"><strong>Status:</strong> Theoretical model · <strong>Target journal:</strong> <i>Musicae Scientiae</i></p>

<div class="pub-meta"><p class="pub-id">MSC-MUS-P02</p><p class="pub-type">Qualitative study</p></div>
<h3 id="msc-mus-p02-title">From personal experience to music</h3>
<p class="pub-question"><strong>Research question:</strong> How do creators describe transforming personal experience into music, including unsuccessful and emotionally neutral attempts?</p>
<div class="pub-body"><div><h4>Scope and methodology</h4><p>The protocol proposes purposive recruitment of approximately 20–30 Polish-speaking adult generator users with varied musical experience. Individual online semi-structured interviews reconstruct the most recent eligible creative episode the participant is willing and able to discuss, including motives, prompting, selection and rejection of versions, perceived agency and personal resonance. Participants may discuss an optional contrasting episode and voluntarily show an excerpt without recording or transferring it; intimate disclosure is not required. Transfer and retention require separate approved procedures and consent. Contextualist reflexive thematic analysis considers divergent experiences, using AIM-ER (AI-assisted Music for Emotion Regulation) as sensitising concepts rather than a compulsory coding scheme.</p></div><div><h4>Scientific significance</h4><p>The study aims to distinguish repeated generation for aesthetic exploration from repetition associated with emotional engagement or frustration. Including accounts without emotional benefits avoids focusing solely on successful tracks. Themes will inform survey wording and the future AI-MRFS instrument, but cannot establish prevalence, clinical effectiveness or causal emotion regulation.</p></div></div>
<div class="pub-constructs"><p class="pub-definition"><strong>Constructs and conceptual meaning:</strong> The study examines the lived process of translating personal experience into a musical artefact, including meaning-making, negotiation of human and system contributions, and perceived consequences. A motive is a reported reason for starting, a function is the role attributed to the activity in an episode, and a consequence is a reported change or absence of change.</p><p class="pub-operationalisation"><strong>Operationalisation and indicators:</strong> The interview corpus is the unit of thematic analysis; an episode is a contextual unit, not an independent observation. If the latest session cannot be discussed, the participant may choose the next most recent one or decline; comfort-related and recall selection limit interpretation. Neutral questions reconstruct initiation, intention, generation, selection, revision, stopping and immediate and later experience. Abandoned and contrasting episodes are included. Reflexive thematic analysis develops patterns of meaning across accounts, retaining ambiguity and counterexamples.</p><p class="pub-interpretation"><strong>Interpretation of the outcome:</strong> Themes are interpretations of experiences, not scale scores or population frequencies. Reported sequences cannot identify causal mediation. Findings may refine the domains of future instruments without forcing conformity with AIM-ER.</p></div>
<div class="pub-parameters"><p class="pub-instruments"><strong>Measurement instruments:</strong> Original semi-structured interview guide (45–60 min), eligibility screener; optional track excerpt. No standardised questionnaire.</p><p class="pub-sample"><strong>Sample size:</strong> 20–30 completed interviews; pilot of 2–3 adults.</p><div class="pub-estimation"><p class="pub-estimate"><strong>Estimation results:</strong> <span class="pub-estimate-count">20–30 interviews</span>. Purposive recruitment targets varied experiences and in-depth thematic analysis; material adequacy will be assessed after piloting. Statistical power does not apply to this qualitative design.</p><p class="pub-estimate-note">Qualitative plan; no empirical results.</p></div></div>
<p class="pub-target"><strong>Status:</strong> Planned study, before data collection · <strong>Target journal:</strong> <i>Psychology of Music</i></p>

<div class="pub-meta"><p class="pub-id">MSC-MUS-P03</p><p class="pub-type">Survey study</p></div>
<h3 id="msc-mus-p03-title">Motives and functions of music creation</h3>
<p class="pub-question"><strong>Research question:</strong> Is a stronger initial emotional-expression motive associated with greater retrospective post-episode emotional clarity?</p>
<div class="pub-body"><div><h4>Scope and methodology</h4><p>A cross-sectional online survey will recruit Polish-speaking adults. Each reconstructs the most recent episode within 30 days, including unfinished or abandoned attempts. Four motives (expression, mood improvement, exploration and entertainment) and recalled pre/post clarity and valence are rated on 0–10 scales. The proposed target is 500 complete eligible primary records, with a conditional candidate cap of 660 submissions or eight weeks, subject to approval after feasibility review. The historical cap of 560 remains a comparison scenario. OLS with HC3 uncertainty adjusts for initial clarity, competing motives, age, musical experience, use frequency, episode recency and recruitment source. Secondary hypotheses concern valence, operation-type diversity (0–3), and agency in relation to personal significance, with Holm correction.</p></div><div><h4>Scientific significance</h4><p>The analysis separates initial motivation, creative operations and perceived consequences. A total of 40,000 synthetic replications examined dependence among motives, rounded ratings and missingness. In one outcome-dependent missingness scenario, rejection at the alternative fell to 39.65%, illustrating how selective nonresponse affects statistical inference. These are synthetic data, not survey findings. Retrospective ratings cannot establish a causal effect of creation.</p></div></div>
<div class="pub-constructs"><p class="pub-definition"><strong>Constructs and conceptual meaning:</strong> Creative motives are intentions preceding an episode: expression, mood improvement, exploration and entertainment. Emotional clarity is reported ease of understanding one's emotions, distinct from valence, the pleasantness of one's state. Agency denotes perceived influence over decisions; personal significance denotes the perceived importance of the episode.</p><p class="pub-operationalisation"><strong>Operationalisation and indicators:</strong> Four motives are rated separately from 0 to 10; clarity and valence are recalled separately before and after the last session. Valence anchors are very unpleasant (0), neutral (5), very pleasant (10). Operation diversity, not the number of iterations, is the sum of regeneration, selection and editing indicators (0–3), requiring all three answers. Functions and difficulties are separate 0–10 ratings.</p><p class="pub-interpretation"><strong>Interpretation of the outcome:</strong> H1 estimates the association of expression motive with post-session clarity at the same baseline clarity and other included covariates, expressed in clarity points per motive point. H2–H4 concern valence, operation diversity and the agency–personal significance association, respectively. A retrospective survey does not identify causal effects.</p></div>
<div class="pub-parameters"><p class="pub-instruments"><strong>Measurement instruments:</strong> Original episode survey: motives, perceived ease of understanding emotions, valence, agency, personal significance, functions and difficulties (0–10); creative operations (yes/no). No selected standardised scale.</p><p class="pub-sample"><strong>Sample size:</strong> 500 complete records (calculated power 91.55%); conditional cap 660 submissions or eight weeks; require at least 95% assurance at a conservative eligibility-and-completeness probability; comprehension check with 8–12 volunteers.</p><div class="pub-estimation"><p class="pub-estimate"><strong>Estimation results:</strong> <span class="pub-estimate-count">60,000 replications and 300 PMM datasets</span>. N=500, partial r=.15: analytical power 91.55%, historical rejection 90.0–92.6% (target 500, varying caps and missingness; 1,000 replications per cell). With mixed MAR, probability of reaching 500 complete records is 81.36% at 640 submissions and 99.10% at 660. At cap 640, MNAR CI coverage was 72.0%; in a separate PMM screen, 76.7%. Cap 660 is a planning candidate: at p=.78 assurance is 92.35%; at p=.75 the rounded candidate is 700, and at p=.70 it is 760. Final cap depends on feasibility review and approval.</p><p class="pub-estimate-note">Planning estimates and simulations, not participant results.</p></div></div>
<p class="pub-target"><strong>Status:</strong> Planned study, before data collection · <strong>Target journal:</strong> <i>Psychology of Music</i></p>

<div class="pub-meta"><p class="pub-id">MSC-MUS-P04</p><p class="pub-type">Randomised online experiment</p></div>
<h3 id="msc-mus-p04-title">Alexithymia and emotional expression</h3>
<p class="pub-question"><strong>Research question:</strong> Does overall alexithymia modify the effect of access to AI emotion-language suggestions on expressive success?</p>
<div class="pub-body"><div><h4>Scope and methodology</h4><p>A planned 1:1 randomised experiment compares eight minutes of assembling a sketch from identical pre-generated instrumental clips, with or without emotion-language suggestions. The moderator is the Polish PAQ-S (six items, total 6–42; no separate facets). The primary score is composite expressive success on a 0–10 scale; confirmed absence of a meaningful sketch is zero, while unknown status or missing applicable rating is missing. Emotional clarity is measured before the task and after suggestions are removed, with an optional 24-hour assessment. H1 uses the assignment-by-PAQ-S interaction, HC3 uncertainty, 50 PMM imputations and 27 missingness sensitivity scenarios.</p></div><div><h4>Scientific significance</h4><p>The study separates assisted expression from unaided emotional understanding. The design includes a proposed 30–50-person pilot. Calibration of 720 synthetic datasets (50 imputations per method) and 3,600 measurement-error datasets highlighted vulnerability to outcome-dependent missingness. These are not participant results. The experiment tests access to a scaffold in this task, not superiority of AI-authored over human-authored language or therapeutic efficacy.</p></div></div>
<div class="pub-constructs"><p class="pub-definition"><strong>Constructs and conceptual meaning:</strong> Alexithymia concerns difficulty identifying and describing emotions and externally oriented thinking, not an absence of emotion. Semantic scaffolding may support mapping language and symbols onto experience without improving unaided emotional understanding. Assisted expression and subjective emotional clarity are therefore distinct.</p><p class="pub-operationalisation"><strong>Operationalisation and indicators:</strong> PAQ-S-PL (six items, sum 6–42) is the baseline moderator; assignment to language suggestions is the experimental factor. Primary expressive success combines production of a meaningful sketch with its 0–10 fit to the intended emotion. Confirmed absence of a meaningful sketch is zero; unresolved status or unavailable rating is missing. Clarity (0–10) is rated before the task and after suggestions are removed. Completion and applicable fit are reported separately.</p><p class="pub-interpretation"><strong>Interpretation of the outcome:</strong> H1 estimates how the assignment effect varies with alexithymia, not a general PAQ-S–expression correlation. An interaction does not establish trait change or objective emotion recognition. The original success composite requires content and comprehension evaluation.</p></div>
<div class="pub-parameters"><p class="pub-instruments"><strong>Measurement instruments:</strong> PAQ-S-PL (6 items, sum 6–42); original fit, clarity and expression-ease ratings (0–10), distress and agency; S-DERS clarity as a candidate.</p><p class="pub-sample"><strong>Sample size:</strong> Pilot 30–50; planning ceiling 1,000 randomised, 800 observed with 20% missingness. Final N undetermined.</p><div class="pub-estimation"><p class="pub-estimate"><strong>Estimation results:</strong> <span class="pub-estimate-count">720 PAQ-S datasets and 3,600 measurement-error datasets</span>. Historical sketch-status-dependent missingness (120 datasets per cell), N=1,000 and a .54-point interaction: PMM gave 89.2% null rejection but 20.0% grid-robust rejection. Enhanced sketch-status capture: 91.7% and 26.7%; at .72 points, robust rejection was 70.8%. Final N undetermined.</p><p class="pub-estimate-note">Planning estimates and simulations, not participant results.</p></div></div>
<p class="pub-target"><strong>Status:</strong> Planned study, before data collection · <strong>Target journal:</strong> <i>Psychology of Music</i></p>

<div class="pub-meta"><p class="pub-id">MSC-MUS-P05</p><p class="pub-type">Psychometric project</p></div>
<h3 id="msc-mus-p05-title">Measuring regulation functions: AI-MRFS</h3>
<p class="pub-question"><strong>Research question:</strong> How can the distinct regulatory functions of AI-assisted music creation be measured reliably?</p>
<div class="pub-body"><div><h4>Scope and methodology</h4><p>The proposed AI-MRFS (AI-Music Regulation Functions Scale) contains 35 original items rated from 0 to 4 for the most recent creative episode within 30 days. Seven provisional domains cover expression, reflection, modulation, diversion, social communication, perseveration and avoidance. Planned stages include review by 6–10 experts, two rounds of cognitive interviews and a 30–50-person pilot. Ordinal exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses will use independent samples. Sample sizes depend on calibration and pilot results. Omega reliability, validity and measurement comparability across musical-experience groups will be examined.</p></div><div><h4>Scientific significance</h4><p>The instrument describes a profile of creative functions rather than therapeutic efficacy; no total score is assumed. Across 72 additional synthetic datasets, seven factors with .40 loadings and .80 correlations yielded 1/12 proper fits (N=500) and 4/12 (N=800), versus 12/12 at N=800 with .35 correlations. Four datasets were compared with lavaan WLSMV. AI-MRFS has no established validity or reliability; N=400 for EFA and N=500 for CFA are separate provisional planning values.</p></div></div>
<div class="pub-constructs"><p class="pub-definition"><strong>Constructs and conceptual meaning:</strong> A regulatory function is the reported role of creative activity during a particular session, independent of initial motives and attainment of desired consequences. Expression, reflection, affect modulation, diversion, social communication, perseveration and avoidance are seven hypothetical domains. Reflection is distinguished from repetitive stuckness, and temporary attentional redirection from avoidance of unwanted experience.</p><p class="pub-operationalisation"><strong>Operationalisation and indicators:</strong> AI-MRFS contains 35 candidate items (five per domain) about the last session within 30 days, rated 0 (not at all) to 4 (to a very great extent). Uncertain responses are missing, not zero. Expert content review and cognitive interviews precede piloting, ordinal exploratory factor analysis and independent confirmation. The model is frozen before CFA.</p><p class="pub-interpretation"><strong>Interpretation of the outcome:</strong> The objective is to establish measurement structure and quality: domain separation, omega reliability, convergent and discriminant validity, and comparability across groups. No total score, norms or clinical thresholds are assumed. Correlations and internal consistency alone do not establish validity.</p></div>
<div class="pub-parameters"><p class="pub-instruments"><strong>Measurement instruments:</strong> Proposed AI-MRFS (35 items, 7 domains, 0–4); candidate validity comparators: MMR/B-MMR, HUMS, ERP-IA-6, rumination and avoidance measures; original motive and clarity ratings.</p><p class="pub-sample"><strong>Sample size:</strong> Pilot 30–50; independent provisional EFA N=400 and CFA N=500 (candidate submission caps 500/600); retest of 100 CFA participants after 7–14 days.</p><div class="pub-estimation"><p class="pub-estimate"><strong>Estimation results:</strong> <span class="pub-estimate-count">72 diagnostic CFA datasets</span>. EFA N=400 and CFA N=500 remain provisional. With .40 loadings and .80 factor correlations, proper fits: 1/12 (8.3%) at N=500, 4/12 (33.3%) at N=800; with .35 correlations and N=800, 12/12. Model stability, not empirical validation.</p><p class="pub-estimate-note">Planning estimates and simulations, not participant results.</p></div></div>
<p class="pub-target"><strong>Status:</strong> Planned study, before data collection · <strong>Target journal:</strong> <i>Assessment</i></p>

<div class="pub-meta"><p class="pub-id">MSC-MUS-P06</p><p class="pub-type">Regulation-process study</p></div>
<h3 id="msc-mus-p06-title">Rumination and avoidance</h3>
<p class="pub-question"><strong>Research question:</strong> Is pre-session motivation to avoid unwanted thoughts or emotions associated with interference in a valued activity during the following day?</p>
<div class="pub-body"><div><h4>Scope and methodology</h4><p>A planned 14-day diary study covers the first eligible natural creative session each day. Within 15 minutes before creation, participants rate avoidance, distress and expected difficulty of a planned activity (0–10). After creation they report distress, brooding and perceived insight; at 24–28 hours they assess interference with the same activity during the fixed first 24 hours. Participant fixed effects and cluster-robust standard errors estimate within-person associations. Secondary hypotheses concern immediate relief, its interaction with avoidance and brooding, with Holm correction.</p></div><div><h4>Scientific significance</h4><p>The design separates immediate relief from later functioning and sad musical content from possible maladaptation. Across 40,200 synthetic replications, the effects of session counts, participant withdrawal and missing responses on statistical inference were investigated. Simulations show that missingness dependent on unobserved outcomes may distort estimates even after weighting. The study does not assume that avoidance or repeated music generation invariably leads to impaired functioning.</p></div></div>
<div class="pub-constructs"><p class="pub-definition"><strong>Constructs and conceptual meaning:</strong> Experiential avoidance is defined here as motivation to move away from unwanted thoughts or feelings, not every attempt at distraction. Brooding is distinguished from perceived new insight, not a measure of the reflection process. Functioning denotes ability to complete a specific previously identified valued activity; immediate relief is not its measure.</p><p class="pub-operationalisation"><strong>Operationalisation and indicators:</strong> Within 15 minutes before creation, assess avoidance, distress and expected activity difficulty (0–10). Within 15 minutes after creation, assess distress, brooding and perceived insight; relief is pre minus post distress. At 24–28 hours, assess interference with the same activity in the fixed first 24 hours (0 none, 10 complete interference), plus completion. Activity identifiers must match. Undefined or unascertained endpoints block full-session numerical imputation. Non-creation days are not sessions.</p><p class="pub-interpretation"><strong>Interpretation of the outcome:</strong> H1 estimates interference points per one-point higher avoidance motive within the same person, adjusting for baseline distress, expected difficulty and study day. H2–H3 concern relief and brooding. Neither causality nor maladaptation can be inferred from the sad character of a track.</p></div>
<div class="pub-parameters"><p class="pub-instruments"><strong>Measurement instruments:</strong> Original diary: avoidance, distress, interference (0–10), brooding, perceived insight and activity completion; RRS-R-PL and BEAQ-PL as candidate baseline instruments.</p><p class="pub-sample"><strong>Sample size:</strong> Final N undetermined; N=160 is a simulation scenario. Fourteen days of observation; feasibility pilot before main study.</p><div class="pub-estimation"><p class="pub-estimate"><strong>Estimation results:</strong> <span class="pub-estimate-count">40,200 replications</span>. N=160, latent slope .15, six sessions and 30% missingness: 95.6% MAR rejection. In a separate MAR-style stress design with 30% attrition: 62.7%, 84.7%, 96.3% for 4, 6, 10 sessions (serial correlation also varied); with 45% attrition and six sessions: 77.7%. N=160 is not an approved target.</p><p class="pub-estimate-note">Planning estimates and simulations, not participant results.</p></div></div>
<p class="pub-target"><strong>Status:</strong> Planned study, before data collection · <strong>Target journal:</strong> <i>Computers in Human Behavior</i></p>

<div class="pub-meta"><p class="pub-id">MSC-MUS-P07</p><p class="pub-type">Ecological momentary assessment</p></div>
<h3 id="msc-mus-p07-title">Emotional changes in everyday life</h3>
<p class="pub-question"><strong>Research question:</strong> Is one-hour valence associated with natural AI creation within that hour among participants with complete windows of both types?</p>
<div class="pub-body"><div><h4>Scope and methodology</h4><p>The 21-day ecological momentary assessment (EMA) protocol proposes one independently scheduled window per day, with baseline, 60-minute and 180-minute assessments. Active creation during the first hour is distinguished from listening alone. Within-person comparisons require observed windows of both exposure types. The primary outcome is one-hour valence (the pleasantness or unpleasantness of the current state), rated from 0 to 10. Secondary outcomes are three-hour valence and deviation from the baseline desired arousal level. The plan accounts for withdrawal, incomplete assessments and Holm correction.</p></div><div><h4>Scientific significance</h4><p>Independently scheduled assessments compare adjusted affect levels in both window types. Across 32,800 synthetic replications, creation prevalence and missingness were investigated. With 45% final-day withdrawal and a latent contrast of 0.4 points, rejection for 320 enrollees was 61.4% at 5% creation prevalence and 88.7% at 10%. The simulations illustrate how infrequent creation limits comparisons between different periods within the same person. The observational design cannot identify causal effects.</p></div></div>
<div class="pub-constructs"><p class="pub-definition"><strong>Constructs and conceptual meaning:</strong> Valence is a bipolar affect dimension from unpleasant (0) to pleasant (10); arousal is activation level. Emotional change is not equated with improved mood: desired arousal depends on the person's goal. The study compares adjusted outcomes at fixed time points, not trajectories or continuous-time inertia.</p><p class="pub-operationalisation"><strong>Operationalisation and indicators:</strong> EMA uses independently scheduled windows over 21 days. Before each window, rate valence, arousal, intention to create and desired arousal (0–10); repeat valence and arousal at 60 and 180 minutes. Exposure is active first-hour creation (yes/no); listening alone is not creation and unknown exposure is not zero. H3 uses absolute deviation between one-hour arousal and baseline desired arousal.</p><p class="pub-interpretation"><strong>Interpretation of the outcome:</strong> H1 compares one-hour valence in creation and non-creation windows within the same person, adjusting for baseline affect, intention and time. H2 concerns three hours. Both window types must be observed for the particular outcome. People without observed switching remain in the flow but do not identify the contrast. Non-creation windows encompass heterogeneous activities. Self-selected creation and contextual factors limit causal interpretation.</p></div>
<div class="pub-parameters"><p class="pub-instruments"><strong>Measurement instruments:</strong> Original EMA: valence, arousal and desired arousal (0–10), creation indicator; no standardised questionnaire.</p><p class="pub-sample"><strong>Sample size:</strong> Pilot 15–25 adults; simulated N=80–320, final N undetermined. Two cognitive-interview rounds of 5–8 adults each.</p><div class="pub-estimation"><p class="pub-estimate"><strong>Estimation results:</strong> <span class="pub-estimate-count">32,800 replications</span>. N=160, 30% planned combined window loss, .4-point contrast: 83.5% and 99.0% rejection at 10% and 25% creation prevalence. In a separate 45% withdrawal scenario, N=320: 61.4% at 5% prevalence, 88.7% at 10%. Final N depends on availability of both window types within individuals.</p><p class="pub-estimate-note">Planning estimates and simulations, not participant results.</p></div></div>
<p class="pub-target"><strong>Status:</strong> Planned study, before data collection · <strong>Target journal:</strong> <i>Psychology of Music</i></p>

<div class="pub-meta"><p class="pub-id">MSC-MUS-P08</p><p class="pub-type">Planned experiment</p></div>
<h3 id="msc-mus-p08-title">Agency and psychological ownership</h3>
<p class="pub-question"><strong>Research question:</strong> How do authority over arrangement decisions and the execution of changes affect agency and whether a track feels like one’s own?</p>
<div class="pub-body"><div><h4>Scope and methodology</h4><p>Equal 1:1:1:1 randomisation in a 2 × 2 design crosses participant or system-rule selection with manual or automatic application. The eight-minute task starts with a 30-second instrumental sketch and includes four arrangement stages (rhythm, harmony, instrumentation, ending), each with two alternatives. Primary ownership is the mean of three 0–10 ratings; agency uses three separate 0–10 ratings, and attributed contribution uses a 0–100 scale.</p></div><div><h4>Scientific significance</h4><p>HC3 regression estimates selection authority and its interaction with execution; H2–H3 use Holm correction. With d=.30, α=.05 and 90% power, the approximate calculation yields 468 complete observations (proposed N=500), without guaranteeing interaction power. Calibration spans N=240/480/720, MCAR/MAR/MNAR and 36 scenarios. Pilot: 30–50 adults. Ownership of an unheard track is undefined; the study does not assess copyright.</p></div></div>
<div class="pub-constructs"><p class="pub-definition"><strong>Constructs and conceptual meaning:</strong> Psychological ownership is the subjective experience that a particular track is mine, represents my work or belongs to my creative activity. Agency is experienced contribution and action-outcome contingency, not factual assigned-role knowledge. These constructs are distinct from contribution, effort, aesthetic preference and legal authorship.</p><p class="pub-operationalisation"><strong>Operationalisation and indicators:</strong> The independent experimental factors are authority to select an option and mode of applying the change. After hearing the referenced arrangement, ownership and agency are separate means of three 0–10 ratings each, requiring all items. Human contribution is rated 0–100; role comprehension is checked after primary ratings. <h3 id="msc-mus-p09-title">Music and perceived event-specific understanding</h3>
<p class="pub-question"><strong>Research question:</strong> Does the musical rather than visual representation package change perceived understanding of one event at day seven?</p>
<div class="pub-body"><div><h4>Scope and methodology</h4><p>A 1:1 randomised comparison pairs a private account of one non-traumatic event from the past 12 months with one of six AI instrumental pieces or one of six nonfigurative images. Both groups have eight minutes. Baseline, immediate and seven-day assessments use the mean of three 0–10 ratings of event-specific self-understanding; psychological distance is rated separately. Text remains local.</p></div><div><h4>Scientific significance</h4><p>H1 concerns understanding at day seven; H2–H3 concern immediate outcomes. The plan includes HC3, Holm correction and 25 MNAR scenarios. A marginal IPW candidate with bootstrap refitting of response models is under calibration for defined but missing outcomes; it has not been approved for confirmatory inference. At d=.30/.35, 90% power and 20% missingness, calculated randomised N values are 588/434, with proposed targets of 600/440. Across 64 synthetic scenarios (200 replications each), MNAR interval coverage fell to 21%. Pilot: 30–50 adults. The contrast estimates a representation-package effect, not an isolated AI effect or global identity change.</p></div></div>
<div class="pub-constructs"><p class="pub-definition"><strong>Constructs and conceptual meaning:</strong> Autobiographical reasoning relates experiences to self-knowledge. The study measures perceived understanding of one event's relation to the current self, values and life story, not the strength of identification with it, global narrative coherence or memory accuracy. Externalisation means pairing a private account with a perceptible symbolic artefact and occurs in both conditions.</p><p class="pub-operationalisation"><strong>Operationalisation and indicators:</strong> At baseline, immediately after the task and seven days later, use the mean of three 0–10 ratings of understanding the event's relation to self, values and life story; all three are required. High understanding may include recognising that the event is not self-defining. Psychological distance, current perceived broader perspective, is a separate 0–10 rating. Importance, distress and resonance are distinct indicators; ratings must refer to the same event.</p><p class="pub-interpretation"><strong>Interpretation of the outcome:</strong> H1 estimates the difference in day-seven understanding points between assigned representation packages, adjusting for baseline. H2 concerns immediate understanding; H3 concerns distance without assuming more is better. The original composite is not a validated narrative identity scale; the contrast does not isolate an AI effect or causal mediator.</p></div>
<div class="pub-parameters"><p class="pub-instruments"><strong>Measurement instruments:</strong> Original event-specific self-understanding (3 × 0–10), psychological distance (0–10), distress, importance and resonance ratings.</p><p class="pub-sample"><strong>Sample size:</strong> Pilot 30–50; proposed N=440 or 600 randomised (d=.35 or .30; 20% loss, power 90.56% or 90.65%); historical HC3 H1 rejection under MAR at N=600: 81.2–99.8%, not current-procedure power. Final N undetermined.</p><div class="pub-estimation"><p class="pub-estimate"><strong>Estimation results:</strong> <span class="pub-estimate-count">32,000 datasets and bootstrap 240 × 199</span>. N=440/600, 20% loss: 352/480 expected observations; power 90.56%/90.65% for d=.35/.30. Historical HC3 across four MAR profiles at a .5-point effect, H1 rejection 67.0–98.2%/81.2–99.8%. Historical IPW at N=600 under H0: CI coverage 77.5% (MAR), 30.0% (MNAR), 40 datasets each. Candidate: 80 × 999 per endpoint; H2–H3 FWER under MNAR, N=600: 20% (4/20), uncertain. Current HC3 cannot provide confirmatory tests with missing outcomes; IPW candidate unapproved. Larger N does not remove selection bias.</p><p class="pub-estimate-note">Planning estimates and simulations, not participant results.</p></div></div>
