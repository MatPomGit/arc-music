# Research programme

[PL](studies.md) · [EN](en-studies.md)

## Creators' experiences

Individual interviews will examine motives for creating music, the circumstances in which tracks are produced and the meanings participants attribute to music in relation to their personal experiences.

## Emotion regulation

A survey will investigate associations between the use of music generators, alexithymia, emotion regulation, rumination and well-being. The proposed AIM-ER (AI-assisted Music for Emotion Regulation) model provides a theoretical basis for hypotheses about the mechanisms underlying these associations.

## Agency and authorship

The programme examines personal contribution to music creation, perceived control over the finished work and changes in affect in everyday life.

<p class="study-status">Study protocols are currently being developed.</p>

<h2 id="publication-title">Research projects</h2>
<p>Nine research projects develop AIM-ER (AI-assisted Music for Emotion Regulation), each addressing a distinct aspect of the psychology of AI-assisted music creation. Empirical studies are at the design stage; simulation findings describe the properties of statistical methods, not participants' experiences.</p>

<article class="pub" id="msc-mus-p01" aria-labelledby="msc-mus-p01-title">
<div class="pub-meta"><p class="pub-id">MSC-MUS-P01</p><p class="pub-type">Theoretical paper</p></div>
<h3 id="msc-mus-p01-title">AIM-ER (AI-assisted Music for Emotion Regulation) model of emotion regulation</h3>
<p class="pub-question"><strong>Research question:</strong> Does emotion regulation depend on the fit between the creator’s goal and the affordances of the music generator?</p>
<div class="pub-body"><div><h4>Scope and methodology</h4><p>The model separates regulatory goals, creative operations (prompting, generation, selection and editing), psychological mechanisms, and immediate and delayed outcomes. Ten falsifiable hypotheses address distress reduction, personal resonance, authorship, agency, alexithymia, rumination, feedback, musical expertise, regulatory flexibility and novelty. A central prediction distinct from H1–H10 is tested in a proposed 2 × 2 experiment crossing goal (emotional articulation or reinterpretation) with interface affordance (preserving meaning or transforming perspective), with comparable duration and generator access.</p></div><div><h4>Scientific significance</h4><p>The primary experimental outcome is goal attainment, with emotional clarity and reinterpretation assessed separately. The framework predicts an advantage for matched conditions, not universal AI effectiveness. A precise null or reversed effect would weaken the prediction. The research programme includes interviews, surveys, diary studies and experiments, separating immediate relief from later functioning and proposed mechanisms from causal evidence.</p></div></div>
<p class="pub-target"><strong>Status:</strong> Theoretical model · <strong>Target journal:</strong> <i>Musicae Scientiae</i></p>

<article class="pub" id="msc-mus-p02" aria-labelledby="msc-mus-p02-title">
<div class="pub-meta"><p class="pub-id">MSC-MUS-P02</p><p class="pub-type">Qualitative study</p></div>
<h3 id="msc-mus-p02-title">From personal experience to music</h3>
<p class="pub-question"><strong>Research question:</strong> How do creators describe transforming personal experience into music, including unsuccessful and emotionally neutral attempts?</p>
<div class="pub-body"><div><h4>Scope and methodology</h4><p>The protocol proposes purposive recruitment of approximately 20–30 Polish-speaking adult generator users with varied musical experience. Individual online semi-structured interviews reconstruct the most recent eligible creative episode, including motives, prompting, selection and rejection of versions, perceived agency and personal resonance. Participants may discuss an optional contrasting episode and voluntarily share an artefact; intimate disclosure is not required. Contextualist reflexive thematic analysis considers divergent experiences, using AIM-ER (AI-assisted Music for Emotion Regulation) as sensitising concepts rather than a compulsory coding scheme.</p></div><div><h4>Scientific significance</h4><p>The study aims to distinguish repeated generation for aesthetic exploration from repetition associated with emotional engagement or frustration. Including accounts without emotional benefits avoids focusing solely on successful tracks. Themes will inform survey wording and the future AI-MRFS instrument, but cannot establish prevalence, clinical effectiveness or causal emotion regulation.</p></div></div>
<p class="pub-target"><strong>Status:</strong> Planned study, before data collection · <strong>Target journal:</strong> <i>Psychology of Music</i></p>
</article>
<article class="pub" id="msc-mus-p03" aria-labelledby="msc-mus-p03-title">
<div class="pub-meta"><p class="pub-id">MSC-MUS-P03</p><p class="pub-type">Survey study</p></div>
<h3 id="msc-mus-p03-title">Motives and functions of music creation</h3>
<p class="pub-question"><strong>Research question:</strong> Is a stronger initial emotional-expression motive associated with greater retrospective post-episode emotional clarity?</p>
<div class="pub-body"><div><h4>Scope and methodology</h4><p>A cross-sectional online survey will recruit Polish-speaking adults. Each reconstructs the most recent episode within 30 days, including unfinished or abandoned attempts. Four motives (expression, mood improvement, exploration and entertainment) and recalled pre/post clarity and valence are rated on 0–10 scales. The proposed target is 480 complete eligible primary records, with a cap of 560 submissions or eight weeks. OLS with HC3 uncertainty adjusts for initial clarity, competing motives, age, musical experience, use frequency, episode recency and recruitment source. Secondary hypotheses concern valence, iterative operations (0–3), and agency in relation to personal resonance, with Holm correction.</p></div><div><h4>Scientific significance</h4><p>The analysis separates initial motivation, creative operations and perceived consequences. A total of 40,000 synthetic replications examined dependence among motives, rounded ratings and missingness. In one outcome-dependent missingness scenario, rejection at the alternative fell to 39.65%, illustrating how selective nonresponse affects statistical inference. These are synthetic data, not survey findings. Retrospective ratings cannot establish a causal effect of creation.</p></div></div>
<p class="pub-target"><strong>Status:</strong> Planned study, before data collection · <strong>Target journal:</strong> <i>Psychology of Music</i></p>
</article>
<article class="pub" id="msc-mus-p04" aria-labelledby="msc-mus-p04-title">
<div class="pub-meta"><p class="pub-id">MSC-MUS-P04</p><p class="pub-type">Randomised online experiment</p></div>
<h3 id="msc-mus-p04-title">Alexithymia and emotional expression</h3>
<p class="pub-question"><strong>Research question:</strong> Does overall alexithymia modify the effect of access to AI emotion-language suggestions on expressive success?</p>
<div class="pub-body"><div><h4>Scope and methodology</h4><p>A planned 1:1 randomised experiment compares eight minutes of assembling a sketch from identical pre-generated instrumental clips, with or without emotion-language suggestions. The moderator is the Polish PAQ-S (six items, total 6–42; no separate facets). The primary score is fit to intended emotion on a 0–10 scale; confirmed absence of a meaningful sketch is zero, while unknown status or missing applicable rating is missing. Emotional clarity is measured before the task and after suggestions are removed, with an optional 24-hour assessment. H1 uses the assignment-by-PAQ-S interaction, HC3 uncertainty, 50 PMM imputations and 27 missingness sensitivity scenarios.</p></div><div><h4>Scientific significance</h4><p>The study separates assisted expression from unaided emotional understanding. The design includes a proposed 30–50-person pilot. Calibration of 720 synthetic datasets (50 imputations per method) and 3,600 measurement-error datasets highlighted vulnerability to outcome-dependent missingness. These are not participant results. The experiment tests access to a scaffold in this task, not superiority of AI-authored over human-authored language or therapeutic efficacy.</p></div></div>
<p class="pub-target"><strong>Status:</strong> Planned study, before data collection · <strong>Target journal:</strong> <i>Psychology of Music</i></p>

<article class="pub" id="msc-mus-p05" aria-labelledby="msc-mus-p05-title">
<div class="pub-meta"><p class="pub-id">MSC-MUS-P05</p><p class="pub-type">Psychometric project</p></div>
<h3 id="msc-mus-p05-title">Measuring regulation functions: AI-MRFS</h3>
<p class="pub-question"><strong>Research question:</strong> How can the distinct regulatory functions of AI-assisted music creation be measured reliably?</p>
<div class="pub-body"><div><h4>Scope and methodology</h4><p>The proposed AI-MRFS (AI-Music Regulation Functions Scale) contains 35 original items rated from 0 to 4 for the most recent creative episode within 30 days. Seven provisional domains cover expression, reflection, modulation, diversion, social communication, perseveration and avoidance. Planned stages include review by 6–10 experts, two rounds of cognitive interviews and a 30–50-person pilot. Ordinal exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses will use independent samples. Sample sizes depend on calibration and pilot results. Omega reliability, validity and measurement comparability across musical-experience groups will be examined.</p></div><div><h4>Scientific significance</h4><p>The instrument aims to describe a profile of creative functions rather than therapeutic efficacy, personality or musical quality. No single total score is assumed. Simulations showed that weak item loadings and highly correlated domains can make the domains difficult to distinguish. In one such dataset, both the project estimator and lavaan detected a problematic factor correlation matrix. AI-MRFS is a proposed research instrument without established validity or reliability.</p></div></div>
<p class="pub-target"><strong>Status:</strong> Planned study, before data collection · <strong>Target journal:</strong> <i>Assessment</i></p>
</article>
<article class="pub" id="msc-mus-p06" aria-labelledby="msc-mus-p06-title">
<div class="pub-meta"><p class="pub-id">MSC-MUS-P06</p><p class="pub-type">Regulation-process study</p></div>
<h3 id="msc-mus-p06-title">Rumination and avoidance</h3>
<p class="pub-question"><strong>Research question:</strong> Is pre-session motivation to avoid unwanted thoughts or emotions associated with interference in a valued activity during the following day?</p>
<div class="pub-body"><div><h4>Scope and methodology</h4><p>A planned 14-day diary study covers the first eligible natural creative session each day. Within 15 minutes before creation, participants rate avoidance, distress and expected difficulty of a planned activity (0–10). After creation they report distress, brooding and reflection; at 24–28 hours they assess interference with the same activity during the fixed first 24 hours. Participant fixed effects and cluster-robust standard errors estimate within-person associations. Secondary hypotheses concern immediate relief, its interaction with avoidance and brooding, with Holm correction.</p></div><div><h4>Scientific significance</h4><p>The design separates immediate relief from later functioning and sad musical content from possible maladaptation. Across 40,200 synthetic replications, the effects of session counts, participant withdrawal and missing responses on statistical inference were investigated. Simulations show that missingness dependent on unobserved outcomes may distort estimates even after weighting. The study does not assume that avoidance or repeated music generation invariably leads to impaired functioning.</p></div></div>
<p class="pub-target"><strong>Status:</strong> Planned study, before data collection · <strong>Target journal:</strong> <i>Computers in Human Behavior</i></p>
</article>
<article class="pub" id="msc-mus-p07" aria-labelledby="msc-mus-p07-title">
<div class="pub-meta"><p class="pub-id">MSC-MUS-P07</p><p class="pub-type">Ecological momentary assessment</p></div>
<h3 id="msc-mus-p07-title">Emotional changes in everyday life</h3>
<p class="pub-question"><strong>Research question:</strong> Do valence and arousal follow different trajectories in natural AI-creation and non-creation windows within the same person?</p>
<div class="pub-body"><div><h4>Scope and methodology</h4><p>The 21-day ecological momentary assessment (EMA) protocol proposes one independently scheduled window per day, with baseline, 60-minute and 180-minute assessments. Active creation during the first hour is distinguished from listening alone. Within-person comparisons require observed windows of both exposure types. The primary outcome is one-hour valence (the pleasantness or unpleasantness of the current state), rated from 0 to 10. Secondary outcomes are three-hour valence and deviation from the baseline desired arousal level. The plan accounts for withdrawal, incomplete assessments and Holm correction.</p></div><div><h4>Scientific significance</h4><p>Independently scheduled assessments allow comparisons of affective change during both creative and non-creative periods. Across 32,800 synthetic replications, creation prevalence and missingness were investigated. With 45% final-day withdrawal and a latent contrast of 0.4 points, power for 320 enrollees was 61.4% at 5% creation prevalence and 88.7% at 10%. The simulations illustrate how infrequent creation limits comparisons between different periods within the same person. The observational design cannot identify causal effects.</p></div></div>
<p class="pub-target"><strong>Status:</strong> Planned study, before data collection · <strong>Target journal:</strong> <i>Psychology of Music</i></p>
</article>

<h3 id="msc-mus-p08-title">Agency and psychological ownership</h3>
<p class="pub-question"><strong>Research question:</strong> How do authority over arrangement decisions and the execution of changes affect agency and whether a track feels like one’s own?</p>
<div class="pub-body"><div><h4>Scope and methodology</h4><p>A 2 × 2 experiment varies who selects an arrangement option and who applies it. Participants or a system rule select the option, while participants or the interface apply it at a shared deadline. All groups receive the same set of instrumental candidates and comparable preview time.</p></div><div><h4>Scientific significance</h4><p>The main outcome is psychological ownership of the version the participant heard. Perceived agency and attributed contribution are measured separately. The design tests whether manually applying a change modifies the effect of being able to choose it.</p></div></div>

<h3 id="msc-mus-p09-title">Music and narrative identity</h3>
<p class="pub-question"><strong>Research question:</strong> How might musical representation of experience relate to the interpretation of one’s life story?</p>
<div class="pub-body"><div><h4>Scope and methodology</h4><p>A planned comparison of autobiographical narratives and their musical representations will consider personal meaning, perspective change, narrative coherence and psychological distance. Analysis will distinguish subjective resonance from literal content fidelity. Sensitive materials will not be required.</p></div><div><h4>Scientific significance</h4><p>An account of potential functions of musical externalisation in meaning-making and narrative identity, without assuming therapeutic efficacy.</p></div></div>
